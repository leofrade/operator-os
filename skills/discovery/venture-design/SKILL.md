# Venture Design

> The only condition a business must satisfy is a paying customer. Everything you produce before that is a bet on finding one faster, and most of it is not worth making.

## The question this answers

- "We have a technology and a vague sense of who wants it. Where do we point it first?"
- "How do I know this market deserves a year before I spend the year?"
- "The pitch says we save money. How much, in whose budget, measured how?"
- "Which planning exercises are load-bearing and which are homework?"

## When to run it

Run this at the start of a venture or at a hard reset — a pivot, a new line inside an existing company, a spinout. It works when you have primary evidence and need a committed direction with numbers attached. Run it as an audit too: in a business that is not growing, walking the gates usually finds the broken one — a beachhead nobody narrowed, a value claim nobody quantified, acquisition cost calculated to hide the truth.

**Do not run this when** you are pre-evidence. This is a design discipline, not a discovery one, and it will structure fiction into a confident plan. Skip the full sequence for a services or local business: much of it exists to justify the capital needs of an innovation-led venture.

## The method

The canonical curriculum runs to two dozen sequenced steps across six themes. That granularity earns its keep in a classroom. In an operating venture, five decisions gate everything; the rest are inputs to them, or artifacts for outsiders.

### Gate 1 — Pick a beachhead narrow enough to dominate

Commit to one segment you intend to own completely. The bar is high: they buy for the same reason, they talk to each other, and one salesperson could reach a meaningful fraction of them. Score candidates on what predicts survival, not on size:

| Criterion | The test |
|---|---|
| Reachable | Can you reach a decision maker without a chain of introductions? |
| Funded and motivated | Budget, plus a reason to act this year rather than eventually? |
| Winnable | Can you deliver a complete solution now, and does winning here open adjacent segments? |

Decision rule: you have not narrowed enough until you can list ten real named prospects who fit. If the list is generic, keep cutting on geography, size, role, or trigger. Size the market bottom-up in one afternoon to answer one question: big enough to be worth a year, small enough to lead.

Skippable when bootstrapped: precision market modeling, follow-on sizing, adjacent-market roadmaps. Those exist for investors who need a billion-dollar story.

### Gate 2 — Quantify the value in the customer's own units

State what the job costs them today — hours, errors, penalties, headcount — then the cost with your product, and subtract. That delta is the value proposition; the rest is adjectives.

Two rules make it usable. Denominate the delta in something they already track — a saving that appears in no number anyone is measured on will not be believed. And pick one or two metrics aligned to the buyer's priorities, not to what your product is good at. Then plan to capture a minority of that value, near a fifth, and expect to defend even that.

Then answer two questions in a sentence each. **What is our core** — the asset that compounds and cannot be copied by shipping the same feature: proprietary data, a network effect, a process nobody else can staff. Being early is not a core. **Where do we sit against the alternative** — on the two axes the customer cares about, status quo plotted. Doing nothing wins most deals; until you beat it, comparing yourself to vendors is premature.

Decision rule: no delta a customer would recognize, no pricing and no selling.

### Gate 3 — Map how the purchase actually happens

Two maps, both required, routinely conflated. **Their process**: everyone who touches the decision — champion, budget owner, influencers, whoever can veto alone, procurement — and what each wants and fears. Then the sequence: what triggers the search, how options get evaluated, which approvals exist, how long each takes.

**Your process**: how you create and close those deals, and what it costs. Four costs are always understated — the loaded cost of selling, cycle length, pre-sale support, post-sale implementation.

Threshold: a cycle beyond roughly twelve months is an existential fact for an early venture, not an inconvenience. If the map says twelve-plus, find a smaller entry purchase in the same account or change segments.

### Gate 4 — Make the unit economics survive contact

Lifetime value is discounted profit over a realistic relationship, not revenue over an optimistic one. Build it from gross margin, use a real retention rate because customers leave regardless of contracts, and discount hard — money in year four is worth a fraction of money now.

Acquisition cost is top-down or it is wrong: total sales and marketing spend in a period divided by new customers won in it. Per-rep math omits failed attempts, overhead, tooling, and management, and flatters.

Decision rule: aim for lifetime value at three times acquisition cost or better. Below that, the gap cannot cover the overhead the model ignores or the optimism in your estimates — fix pricing, retention, or the model, but do not build. Early on, acquisition cost above value is normal; what matters is a credible mechanism by which it falls: referrals, inbound, shorter cycles, expansion revenue.

Skippable when bootstrapped: surveying the full taxonomy of monetization structures. Pick the one procurement can absorb without a fight.

### Gate 5 — Ship a minimum product that takes money

The minimum version satisfies three conditions at once, and teams drop the second:

1. The customer gets real value from using it.
2. The customer **pays** for it.
3. It produces enough feedback to make the next version better.

A free pilot satisfies one and three and teaches you nothing about demand. Payment is the experiment. Before building, list the assumptions the plan depends on, rank them by damage-if-wrong, and design the cheapest test for the top few — several need no code.

Exit criterion: evidence that real customers adopt, use repeatedly, pay, and refer, with numbers improving across successive groups. Enthusiasm without payment does not clear it.

## Failure modes

**The beachhead that was never narrowed** — the team names a vertical and calls it a market. Feedback comes back contradictory because three buyer types are being averaged. → Force the ten-names test. Without ten real prospects sharing a trigger and a budget owner you have a category, and more interviews will not fix it.

**The unquantified value proposition** — "we make the process more efficient." No baseline, no delta, no unit. It survives internally because everyone believes it and nobody defends it to a buyer. → No number, no build. Take the current cost from a customer's own records and write the subtraction.

**Mistaking features for a core** — the defensibility slide lists capabilities and being first. Both are copyable within a year by anyone who finds your market attractive. → Ask what gets stronger every month you operate and cannot be bought. If the answer is nothing yet, say so and design a core deliberately.

**Bottom-up acquisition cost** — a plausible per-deal figure from rep time and ad spend, landing two to four times below reality and justifying a plan that runs out of cash. → Divide total sales and marketing spend by new customers won: salaries, commissions, tools, events, agencies, the founder's selling time. The uncomfortable number is the real one.

**Gate-passing as progress** — every artifact complete and reviewed, no revenue. Finishing a framework feels like building a company. → Each gate must end in a changed decision or new evidence. The process is a spiral: a team that never revisits an earlier gate is not learning.

## Outputs

1. A **beachhead brief**: segment defined by trigger, role, and budget owner, plus ten named prospects.
2. A **quantified value statement** with sources, the core in one sentence, and a two-axis position chart.
3. A **buying map**: decision roles with veto points, the customer's process with stage durations, your sales motion and its loaded cost.
4. A **unit economics model**: lifetime value from gross margin, acquisition cost top-down, the ratio, and how it improves.
5. A **minimum paid product definition**: three conditions with evidence, the ranked assumptions, and the test for the top one.
6. An **honest skip list** of what this venture deliberately will not produce.

## Lineage

Bill Aulet's *Disciplined Entrepreneurship* argues that entrepreneurship is a teachable craft rather than a temperament, and gives it a curriculum: one primary market, a specific persona, value quantified against the current state, an explicit decision-making unit, unit economics that clear a ratio, and a minimum product that must be paid for. The insistence that a business exists only when someone pays is the spine of everything above, and the beachhead-then-adjacent sequencing owes a debt to Geoffrey Moore.

This version compresses that sequence into the five decisions gating capital and calendar, and names which remaining artifacts serve investors rather than operators. It loses pedagogical completeness on purpose: a bootstrapped team executing five gates well beats one producing two dozen documents.

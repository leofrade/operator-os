# Founder Patterns

> The useful part of founder stories is not the story. It is that the same few structural situations recur, and each has a tell you can check today.

## The question this answers

- Are the signals telling me to pivot, or am I just tired?
- Is this cofounder complementary, or merely agreeable?
- Where did my users actually come from, versus where I say they came from?
- Is this market late, or is it not coming?

## When to run it

Run it where founder judgment does work that data cannot: before committing another
two quarters to a direction, when picking or confronting a cofounder, after an
unexplained growth spike, in a quarter where payroll is in doubt. Run it annually as
a self-audit even when things are calm.

**Do not run this when** you have a specific, answerable question. Pattern matching
against other companies' histories is the weakest evidence available and is
seductive because it is cheap. If the question is "will this segment pay," run an
experiment. Also do not run it to justify a decision already made — the pattern
library is large enough to find a precedent for anything, which is the main way this
material does damage.

## The method

### 1. Score the pivot signal panel

Five signals recur where a pivot turned out to be right. Score each present or
absent using evidence from the last 60 days.

| Signal | The tell | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Ghost user | People use the product for something you did not design | Decisive |
| Side feature outgrows core | A demo or secondary feature gets the engagement | Decisive |
| Forced evangelism | Every user requires personal persuasion to adopt | Strong |
| The arriving market | Your thesis depends on a change imminent for over a year | Strong |
| Adjacent problem | You keep finding a more urgent problem next to yours | Moderate |

Decision rule: either decisive signal present, pivot now — the market has told you
what it wants and you are declining to hear it. Two or more of the others, run a
two-week test of the alternative first. None present and growth still flat: the
problem is execution or distribution, not direction, and a pivot destroys whatever
compounding you have.

The distinction that matters: a pivot moves toward evidence that already exists.
Changing direction because the current one is hard, with no observed pull anywhere
else, is a restart with a worse cap table.

### 2. Stress-test the cofounder relationship for correlation, not talent

The usual evaluation asks whether the person is smart and skilled. Wrong axis: that
is table stakes. What breaks companies is **correlated failure** — two people who
lose confidence in the same month and are blind to the same risks.

Five questions, answered from real history, not intention:

1. At your lowest point last year, what did they do? (Both at the lowest point
   simultaneously is itself the finding.)
2. What class of decision do you defer to them on without arguing?
3. Have they told you something you did not want to hear, unprompted, this quarter?
4. What is the opportunity cost of their being here, and do they mention it?
5. If this fails in eighteen months, what do they do next — and did they have that
   plan before joining?

The build/sell split is the robust configuration, not for skill coverage but because
the two roles fail on different schedules. A build-only pair ships something nobody
is told about; a sell-only pair sells something that does not exist. Same strengths
and same anxieties means validating each other into a wall.

Commitment asymmetry is the quiet killer. One person full-time and one helping on
weekends is unpaid contracting with equity attached, and it produces a resentment
negotiation when you can least afford one. Resolve it in writing early.

### 3. Audit where distribution actually came from

Most founders cannot explain their own early growth, so they cannot repeat it. Go
back to the first 100 users and classify each by real origin, not by attribution
model. Three mechanisms recur; only one is common:

| Mechanism | Condition it requires | What it looks like when fake |
|---|---|---|
| The product carries itself | It is used in interaction with non-users, so use is exposure | A share button, called viral |
| Novelty spread | It provokes a reaction strong enough to be worth forwarding | One spike, then nothing, treated as a channel |
| Network utility | Each user makes the product more useful to existing users | Network effects claimed at 200 users |

The test that settles it: **could you stop the growth if you tried?** Genuine
mechanisms are hard to switch off — you manage demand rather than manufacture it. If
growth requires you to personally ask, it is sales. Fine and honorable, but cost and
staff it as sales; do not project it as a loop.

### 4. Name the structural change, with a date

Timing beats insight, and timing is testable. Complete this sentence with something
that happened, and when: *This is possible or necessary now because [structural
change] happened in [month, year].* Acceptable: a cost curve crossing a threshold,
a regulation taking effect, a platform opening, a behavior becoming default.
Unacceptable: "people are becoming more aware of."

If your answer is a change that has not happened yet, you are early, and early is
indistinguishable from wrong on a balance sheet. It is survivable only with a
business that pays its own way while it waits.

### 5. Convert constraints into a scope decision before they are forced on you

Limits improve products by forcing cuts that would otherwise be argued about
forever, and you do not need to be poor to get the benefit. Ask: if the budget
halved on Monday, what would you cut? Cut most of it now, while the choice is yours.
That list is ranked by conviction rather than by who asked loudest, which makes it
the better roadmap.

### 6. Read existential threats on the derivative, not the level

Threats that kill companies — fraud, abuse, unit-economics inversion, support load
scaling faster than revenue — get dismissed while they are still cheap to fix,
because their absolute value looks tolerable. Measure the problem's growth rate
against the company's. A loss category at 1% but doubling monthly while revenue
grows 20% is fatal and looks like a rounding error. Plot it as a rate, on the
revenue dashboard.

## Failure modes

**Idea monogamy** — attachment to the solution rather than the problem. Tell: you
describe your product in detail and the customer's problem in adjectives. → Write the
problem statement without naming your product. If it does not survive, you were
defending an artifact.

**Persuasion mistaken for market education** — every deal closes only after a
personal masterclass, framed as being early in a category. Tell: nobody has ever
bought without you in the room. → Category creation still produces buyers who arrive
convinced by their own pain. Test with one prospect you do not handle.

**Correlated cofounders** — same skills, same optimism, same blind spots. Tell: no
substantive disagreement about priority, ever. → Agreement usually signals
insufficient coverage. Give one person ownership of the case against the plan.

**Pattern cosplay** — copying the surface tactics of famous companies. Tell: the
plan cites what a well-known founder did rather than what your customers did last
week. → These histories are survivorship-biased and the visible tactic is rarely the
causal mechanism. Use patterns as diagnostics on your own evidence, not as a
template.

**Rejection as confirmation** — every "no" becomes evidence of being ahead of the
time. Tell: you have a story for why each rejection proves the thesis. → Separate the
sources. Investor rejection is weak evidence. Rejection by users who have the problem
is the strongest negative signal available; ignoring it is not contrarian, only
expensive.

**Optics over output** — energy going into looking like a real company. Tell: more
time in internal review than in front of users. → Cut every ritual that does not
change a decision. Nobody buys because the reporting was tidy.

## Outputs

- A scored pivot panel with the decision rule applied, and the two-week test defined
  if it triggers
- A cofounder assessment covering failure correlation and commitment symmetry, with
  any asymmetry resolved in writing
- A first-100-users origin audit naming the real acquisition mechanism per user
- A timing statement naming the structural change and its date, or an admission of
  being early plus the funding plan that survives it
- A halved-budget scope list, used as the next roadmap
- A rate-based watchlist of existential threats, plotted alongside revenue

## Lineage

Jessica Livingston's *Founders at Work* collected long-form interviews with founders
about their early days, before outcomes made the decisions look inevitable. Its
contribution is not advice but variance: how often the original idea was wrong, how
ordinary the origins were, how much of what is later called strategy was a response
to evidence nobody planned for. Where this articulation departs: an anthology cannot
be executed, and the temptation is to read it as inspiration. I keep only the
regularities that produce a checkable tell and discard the anecdotes, because the
anecdote is the part that does not transfer.

# Customer Development

> Companies rarely die of a missing feature. They die of hiring a sales team against a sales process that was never proven to exist.

## The question this answers

- Have we earned the right to spend on growth, or are we buying activity?
- The product is finished and not selling — what stage are we actually in?
- What kind of market are we in, and what does that forbid?
- What should release the next tranche of capital: a ship date, or a customer fact?

## When to run it

Run it when you are about to convert learning spend into execution spend: a first
sales hire, a paid acquisition budget, a launch, a Series A plan. Run it when a
built product is failing to sell and the instinct in the room is to replace the
sales leader. Run it at formation, to set the milestones the board holds you to.

**Do not run this when** you already have a documented sales process a second
person can execute, predictable unit economics, and a channel that fills pipeline
without founders. That is a scaling problem, not a discovery one. Also do not run
it as a substitute for interview craft — technique lives in `customer-interviews`.
This skill governs sequence, gates, and organization.

## The method

The four stages below run in order and are gated. Each is a loop: you cycle inside
a stage until it passes, and returning to a previous stage is expected behavior,
not failure. One step in this method is not a stage — classifying your market type
— and it is set out between stages 1 and 2 because that is when you need it and
because getting it wrong contaminates everything after. Two rules govern all of
them. Keep burn low through stages 1 and 2 — revenue bought with cash cannot be
distinguished from revenue earned by fit. And never let a ship date function as a
company milestone; it says engineering finished, not that anyone will buy.

### 1. Discovery — turn the plan into claims, then go find who has the pain

Write down what you believe, in falsifiable form: who has the problem, what it
costs them today, what they use instead, who signs, what price holds. Then leave
the building and test the problem before the solution. You are not collecting
feature requests or surveying the market. You are looking for a small group whose
pain is severe enough that they will buy something unfinished.

Rank prospects on buying readiness:

| Rung | State | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Has the problem, does not know it | Ignore. |
| 2 | Knows, tolerates it | Future mainstream buyer, not now. |
| 3 | Actively searching, has a deadline | Warm, but not your first customer. |
| 4 | Built a workaround — a spreadsheet, a manual process, a contractor | Your buyer. |
| 5 | Workaround *and* budget they control or can pull forward | First customer, evangelist, future advisor. |

Only rungs 4 and 5 count. The homemade workaround is the highest-signal artifact
in discovery: pain expressed in someone's own labor. It tells you what the job
is, and what they already spend maintaining it is your price ceiling.

**Gate to pass:** the problem is documented with field evidence, five or more
rung-4-or-5 prospects are named, they confirm the solution concept is sufficient,
and the model produces margin on paper at a price somebody stated out loud.

### Not a stage — classify the market type before spending anything on demand

There is no gate here and nothing to pass. It is a classification you make once
discovery has produced enough field evidence to support one, and it sits before
validation because it changes the go-to-market, the funding profile, and how long
you wait. Getting it wrong is the most expensive error in the method, because each
type punishes the others' playbook.

| Market type | Customers | You compete on | Main risk | Time to traction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New product, existing market | Known, already buying | Superiority against a defined benchmark | Incumbents outspending you | Weeks to months |
| New market | Do not exist yet | Convincing people the category is real | Adoption never arrives | Often years |
| Resegment, low cost | Existing, overserved | Good enough, much cheaper | Incumbent price response | Medium |
| Resegment, niche | A slice of an existing market | Fit to needs incumbents ignore | Incumbent defends a profitable base | Medium |

The operational consequence: in an existing market, brand and paid demand work
because buyers know what to search for. In a new market the same spend is a
donation — nobody searches for a category with no name — and the budget belongs
in education, reference customers, and patience. Revisit the classification;
discovering you got it wrong is one of discovery's most valuable outputs.

### 2. Validation — sell it yourself until the process is written down

The deliverable is not revenue. It is a **repeatable sales roadmap**: the named
buyer, the trigger that starts the deal, the sequence of conversations, the
objections and the answers that work, the price, the cycle length, the channel.
Founders sell here, because you cannot learn a process from someone else's report.

Two exit tests, both required. **The money test:** real purchase orders from
customers you did not personally befriend. Compliments, unconverted pilots, and
letters of intent with no date are not validation. **The repeatability test:**
hand the roadmap to one person who is not a founder. If they close with it, the
process exists. If not, what you have is founder charisma, which does not scale
and cannot be hired for.

Fail either test and you return to discovery. That is the checkpoint the method
exists to enforce.

### 3. Demand creation — spend, indexed to market type

Only now does heavy marketing spend make sense, and only in the shape your market
type allows. The gate: pipeline is generated by something other than the founder's
network, and acquisition cost is measured against a payback period you can survive.

### 4. Company building — replace the discovery organization with an execution one

The everyone-does-everything team that found the market is not the organization
that exploits it. Install functions, hire leaders with domain experience, convert
learned processes into standard ones. Hire in this order: the roadmap, then the
person who runs it, then the team. The common inversion — hire the leader and
expect them to invent the roadmap — is how the death spiral starts.

## Failure modes

**Premature scaling** — a sales team, a marketing hire, and an office are added
because the plan said month nine. Tell: headcount grew before a non-founder closed
using a written process. → Freeze hiring, return to validation. The cost of being
late to scale is weeks; the cost of being early is the company.

**The death spiral** — targets are missed, the sales leader is replaced, the new
one changes strategy, marketing is replaced, then the CEO. Tell: a third straight
quarter of misses explained as an execution problem. → Sales leadership is almost
never the cause. Repeated misses mean the validation gate was never passed.

**Market-type denial** — a new-market company runs an existing-market playbook,
buys ads against keywords nobody types, and concludes the market is bad. Tell:
many impressions, no qualified conversations. → Reclassify, then rebuild the plan
and the runway assumption around education rather than capture.

**Requirements laundering** — discovery becomes a feature-request queue and the
roadmap fills with items requested by people who never bought. Tell: the backlog
grows after every customer call. → The unit of discovery is a validated problem
with a buyer attached, not a feature list.

**Ship date as milestone** — the board deck tracks releases, not customer facts.
Tell: nobody can say which customer claim was proven this month. → Rewrite
milestones as customer milestones: problem validated, workaround found, first
paid order, second close by a non-founder.

**Outsourced learning** — an agency or a junior PM does the customer work and the
founders read the deck. Tell: whoever makes resource decisions has not spoken to a
buyer this month. → Conviction does not transfer through documents. The
decision-maker sits in the calls.

**Gate theater** — stages are declared complete because the calendar moved. Tell:
"we finished discovery in Q1" with no artifacts. → Each gate needs inspectable
evidence. Treat re-entry as progress, or the team learns to fake exits.

## Outputs

- A written hypothesis set: buyer, problem, workaround, economic buyer, price,
  channel, sales cycle
- A market-type classification with its go-to-market and runway implications
- A ranked prospect list with buying-readiness rungs and observed workarounds
- A gate scorecard: current stage, evidence present, evidence missing
- A sales roadmap detailed enough for a non-founder to execute
- A hiring sequence tied to gates, with a stated freeze condition
- Customer milestones for board reporting, replacing product ship dates

## Lineage

Steve Blank's *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* argued that a startup is not a small
version of a large company and should not run product development as its master
schedule. It introduced the four-stage sequence, a customer-facing learning process
running parallel to engineering, the earlyvangelist, and the observation that market
type determines strategy. Where this articulation departs: the original is a
comprehensive program most teams never finish, and its stage names get used as
labels rather than gates. I keep only the gates and make them adversarial — the
repeatability test, handing the sales roadmap to someone who is not a founder, is
what separates companies ready to scale from companies that merely feel ready.

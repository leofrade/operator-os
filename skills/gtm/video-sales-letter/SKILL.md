# Video Sales Letter

> Video sells by installing a reason to believe, not by generating excitement; the direct-response canon is right about the first and badly wrong about how much of the second a considered buyer tolerates.

## The question this answers

- How long should this video be, and what decides that other than taste?
- Why does the hook get watched and nothing converts after twenty seconds?
- Which parts of the direct-response playbook burn credibility with an enterprise buyer?

## When to run it

Run this when video is load-bearing: paid acquisition where creative is the targeting mechanism, a landing page carrying the argument with no human present, a recorded demo a champion forwards internally, or a founder video doing the work of a first call at scale.

**Do not run this when** the offer or positioning is unresolved. Video is the most expensive medium in which to discover your claim is not believable; the same discovery costs a paragraph in an email. Settle [`gtm/offer-design`](../../gtm/offer-design/SKILL.md) first. Skip it where relationship-led selling across a few accounts makes better calls the real fix.

## The method

### Move 1 — Diagnose before you write a word

**Awareness.** Does the viewer know they have the problem? Know solutions exist? Know yours? Most brands write only for the slice already comparing vendors, which is why their video works only in retargeting.

**Saturation.** Pull the top ten competitors' claims. If everyone promises one outcome in different words, an outcome-led video is invisible. If competitors name proprietary methods, you need a mechanism, not a better adjective. If reviews call the whole category useless, only identity and proof move anything.

| Awareness × saturation | Approach | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware, saturated | Story or contrarian claim, product late | 20 min+ |
| Problem-aware, mechanism-led competition | The cause, then the method | 10-25 min |
| Solution-aware, moderate competition | Comparison, differentiation, proof | 5-15 min |
| Vendor-comparing, ready | Offer, terms, proof, next step | 2-6 min |

In a committee purchase, stakeholders sit at different points on that grid at once. The answer is several short videos, one per seat, not one long video serving none.

**Exit criterion:** both readings written down, and a length taken from the table.

### Move 2 — Build the mechanism pair

Two claims, in order. Why previous attempts failed — a cause the viewer has not considered, which converts self-blame into relief. Then why this approach works where those did not. Without the pair you assert superiority, as does everyone else.

Validate against three tests: explainable in two sentences to an outsider; interesting enough to repeat to a colleague; supported both intuitively and empirically, with a number, study, or documented case behind it.

Naming it turns the mechanism into property, and turns it against you when the name has nothing underneath. In consumer categories invented terminology mostly survives contact. In B2B a technical evaluator takes it apart in the second meeting, and vocabulary wrapped around an ordinary feature hands a competitor a story about your honesty.

**Exit criterion:** a named mechanism your technical lead would defend in review.

### Move 3 — Write the lead by awareness, not by habit

The lead is the first sixty to ninety seconds of a short video, or five to ten minutes of a long one. It is not the hook: the hook is three seconds and exists only to stop the scroll. Conflating them produces videos that open loudly and go nowhere.

| Awareness | Lead type | Opens with |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Story | A specific scene and consequence, no product |
| Problem-aware | Problem and cost | The pain, sharply, with the number attached |
| Solution-aware | Mechanism | Why the standard approach fails structurally |
| Vendor-comparing | Offer | Terms, proof, and the next step |

Write for one person, in speech, in their vocabulary, then read it aloud. Anything you would not say to their face gets cut — that filter alone removes most of what does not transfer from consumer direct response.

**Exit criterion:** a lead that survives being read aloud to someone in the target role.

### Move 4 — Engineer the offer, then translate it for the sale you run

Structure the offer as components with defensible worth, handle risk directly, state one next action. Then translate: the direct-response offer stack was built for impulse purchases funded by the buyer's own money, and it inverts when the buyer must defend the purchase internally.

| Consumer device | What it does in a B2B sale | Use instead |
|---|---|---|
| Countdown timers, "3 spots left" | Manipulative; the champion stops forwarding | A real constraint: implementation capacity, cohort start, budget cycle |
| Anchoring and price slashing | Signals the list price was fictional | One defensible price, value gap shown |
| Unconditional money-back | They want the year back, not the money | A scoped pilot with success criteria and a clean exit |
| Secrecy and revelation framing | Destroys technical credibility on contact | Named mechanism plus a documented case |
| Income and outcome claims | Legal exposure and instant disbelief | One customer's measured result, attributed |

**Exit criterion:** every device in the script is one a rep would say out loud.

### Move 5 — Produce modularly, test structurally

Treat every video as three interchangeable layers: hook, body, call to action. Film five or more hooks per shoot; they carry the most variance in the finished asset and cost minutes on set.

Test a spread — three different concepts, two headline styles, two body lengths — not one hero asset. The point is the winning element, not the winning ad. Variants must differ in message or format, or the platform treats them as duplicates.

Refresh on leading indicators, which decay in order: three-second view rate falls first, then hold rate, then click-through, then efficiency. My operating defaults are click-through down 25 to 30 percent from the asset's own first-week baseline, or frequency above 3. These are not published benchmarks — they are the numbers that have worked for me, and they are wrong for someone whose audience is smaller or whose buying cycle is longer. Set your own by recording each asset's first-week rates, then watching where the return-on-spend curve actually broke on the last three assets you retired. Waiting for return on spend to drop means funding a dead asset for two weeks.

**Exit criterion:** a hook library, a cadence, and a refresh threshold written as a number.

### Move 6 — Diagnose down the attention funnel, in order

Do not debate the script. Fix the first broken link.

```
3s view rate low             → the hook failed. Body is irrelevant.
3s fine, 15s rate low        → the lead failed. Wrong type, or too slow.
Hold fine, click-through low → the offer or the call to action failed.
Clicks fine, conversion low  → the landing page contradicts the video.
Conversion fine, quality low → the hook recruits the wrong person.
```

The last line is the one B2B teams miss: a hook that attracts individual contributors when you sell to executives produces excellent creative metrics and worthless pipeline for a quarter before anyone notices. Report three-second rate next to qualified conversion, same slide, always.

## Failure modes

**Consumer theater in an enterprise sale** — urgency, scarcity, and revelation framing aimed at a buying committee. The tell is a champion who watches and does not forward. → Apply the Move 4 table before scripting, not after the bad quarter.

**Mechanism as vocabulary** — a capitalized proprietary name over an ordinary feature. The tell is nobody outside marketing able to explain it. → If your technical lead will not defend it, it is not a mechanism.

**Feature tour dressed as a demo video** — thirty minutes of interface narration ending in "any questions." → Curate to the three to five capabilities that prove the mechanism, and close with a specific ask.

**Single-winner mindset** — one video, endlessly polished, dead in three weeks with nothing behind it. → Ship a portfolio; fatigue is arithmetic, not failure.

**Unsourced return-on-investment math** — savings built on assumptions the buyer never agreed to. The tell is finance rebuilding your model and reaching half the number. → Use the buyer's inputs, show the formula, lead with the conservative case.

## Outputs

- A diagnosis: awareness, saturation stage, claim audit, chosen length.
- The mechanism pair, named, with its two layers of support.
- A lead script matched to awareness, read-aloud tested.
- An offer with the B2B translation applied line by line.
- A modular script in hook, body, and CTA blocks, with five to ten hook variants per concept drawn from `references/hook-patterns.md`.
- A test plan with a refresh threshold, and the funnel reading with the first broken link named.

## Lineage

The diagnostic spine is Eugene Schwartz's, from *Breakthrough Advertising*: awareness and market sophistication as the two readings that decide what a message is allowed to say. The research-then-mechanism-then-brief-then-copy sequence follows Stefan Georgi's RMBC method, whose contribution is moving work upstream until writing becomes assembly. Long-form structure comes from Jon Benson, belief-dismantling from Russell Brunson, the Big Idea from Todd Brown, and behind it Gary Halbert and John Carlton.

Two departures. The canon was built for people spending their own money on impulse, and this version treats much of its offer machinery as harmful where a purchase must be defended internally — the Move 4 table is the operative disagreement, not a footnote. And where the canon treats a named mechanism as a creative act, this treats it as a claim carrying an evidentiary burden, because in B2B the person who takes it apart holds a veto.

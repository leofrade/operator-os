# Hook Patterns

A hook has one job: buy the next three seconds. It does not sell, qualify, or explain. Judge it on whether the viewer is still there at second four, and nothing else.

Every hook is two layers operating together — a **visual** layer (the first frame and the motion in it) and a **verbal** layer (the first spoken or written line). Strong hooks put the layers in counterpoint rather than in agreement: the picture raises a question the line does not immediately answer, or the line contradicts what the picture implies. Two layers saying the same thing is one layer with extra cost.

Produce five to ten per concept. Filming an extra hook costs a few minutes on set; discovering you have only one costs a month.

---

## Visual pattern families

Eight families. Pick from different families when generating a spread — five variations inside one family is one hypothesis wearing five hats.

### 1. Withheld information

Something is deliberately obscured and resolves on screen: a blurred frame sharpening, a cover being removed, a partial view widening.

- **Template:** open on an obscured state that implies a number or outcome, resolve it inside two seconds.
- **Why it works:** an unresolved visual creates a small involuntary pull toward resolution. It is pre-attentive and does not require the viewer to have decided anything yet.
- **Fails when:** resolution takes longer than three seconds, or the resolved state is mundane. A curiosity gap that opens onto something ordinary trains the viewer to skip your next one.
- **Considered purchase:** a chaotic working state resolving into an organized one. Avoid physical gimmickry — sticky notes and hand-drawn signs read as low-budget to a buyer evaluating a six-figure commitment.

### 2. Broken physics

Motion that violates expectation: reversed footage, objects assembling themselves, a state change that could not happen in that order.

- **Template:** film the ordinary sequence, then invert it.
- **Why it works:** the visual system flags anomalies before conscious attention engages. Cheap to produce, effective on any surface.
- **Fails when:** the trick is the entire idea and the product never earns the attention. Also fails for subjects where levity undercuts the claim — security, finance, anything where the buyer's job is on the line.
- **Considered purchase:** scattered documents assembling into a structured record; a backlog draining in reverse.

### 3. Reverse chronology

Open on the finished outcome, then move backward toward the starting condition.

- **Template:** show the resolved end state in frame one, then travel back through how it got there.
- **Why it works:** it inverts the default order, and the gap between visible outcome and unknown process is exactly the gap the body of your video fills.
- **Fails when:** the process is too complex to compress, and the rewind becomes a montage of nothing.
- **Considered purchase:** a completed audit, close, or certification rewinding to the mess that preceded it.

### 4. Contradiction

The speaker says something an advertiser is not supposed to say: an admitted error, a warning against buying, a reversal of the category's received wisdom.

- **Template:** "I was wrong about [common belief], and it cost us [specific consequence]."
- **Why it works:** it violates the expected shape of an advertisement, which is what makes it register as information rather than as a pitch.
- **Fails when:** the admitted error makes you look incompetent rather than candid, or when the "contrarian" position is one everyone already holds. Contrarianism against a straw man is the most common failure in this family.
- **Considered purchase:** works well when the error is strategic rather than operational — a hiring or sequencing mistake a peer would recognize, not a product defect.

### 5. Disqualification

Explicitly exclude most of the audience.

- **Template:** "This is only for [narrow role in specific situation]. If that is not you, skip it."
- **Why it works:** exclusion signals the message was built for a specific person, and specificity is the scarcest quality in a feed. It also improves lead quality by filtering before the click rather than after.
- **Fails when:** the excluded group is most of your market, or the named group is so broad that the exclusion is theater.
- **Considered purchase:** the strongest family here. Naming a role, a company size, and a specific recurring pain outperforms nearly everything else, and the traffic it loses is traffic that was never going to buy.

### 6. Recognition

Name the viewer's state so precisely that they feel observed.

- **Template:** "Does [recurring situation] feel like [uncomfortably accurate comparison]?"
- **Why it works:** hearing your own condition described accurately by a stranger creates an immediate obligation to keep listening.
- **Fails when:** the description is generic ("busy professionals"), or when it is so uncomfortable the viewer leaves to avoid the feeling.
- **Considered purchase:** describe the process failure, not the person. The Monday-morning reconciliation, the quarterly scramble, the report nobody trusts. Never imply the viewer is bad at their job — they are watching at work.

### 7. Instruction

Position as a teacher: the mistakes being made, the statistic nobody knows, the shortcut that removes the unpleasant step.

- **Template:** "Three mistakes [role] make with [process]" or "[N] percent of [role] lose [resource] to [cause]."
- **Why it works:** loss framing outperforms gain framing, and a specific number implies someone counted.
- **Fails when:** the mistakes are obvious, or the statistic has no source. Unsourced numbers are the fastest way to lose a technical viewer permanently.
- **Considered purchase:** works well, on the condition that the statistic is real and attributed on screen. Cite it in frame; the citation itself is a credibility signal.

### 8. Evidence

Lead with scale, peers, or a measured result before saying anything else.

- **Template:** open on the number of users, the peer logos, or the specific outcome one customer got.
- **Why it works:** it reduces perceived risk before the pitch begins, which matters most in categories where being wrong is expensive.
- **Fails when:** the numbers are unremarkable, or the product is new enough that scale claims invite scrutiny. A small number stated confidently beats an inflated one.
- **Considered purchase:** peer proof beats volume proof. Five recognizable companies in the viewer's exact segment outperform a large aggregate user count.

## Verbal patterns

Every spoken hook reduces to one of four moves. Generate one of each when building a spread.

| Move | Shape | Best against |
|---|---|---|
| Reversal | "I believed [X] for years. I was wrong." | Skeptical viewers who distrust confident sellers |
| Loss | "Stop doing [X] if you want [Y]" / "[N] things costing you [Z]" | Viewers who already feel the pain but have not priced it |
| Effort removal | "The low-effort way to [outcome] without [dreaded step]" | Overloaded viewers; weak where effort signals value |
| Exclusion | "This is only for [narrow role]" | Any considered purchase with a defined buyer |

## Anti-patterns

1. **Looks like an ad in frame one.** Logo, polish, and a voiceover cadence the platform has trained viewers to skip.
2. **Setup longer than three seconds.** Whatever the payoff is, it arrives after the viewer is gone.
3. **Curiosity with no payoff.** The gap opens and nothing fills it. Costs future hooks, not just this one.
4. **No text overlay.** Most feed viewing is silent. A hook only spoken is a hook only some people receive.
5. **Text outside the safe zone.** Platform interface elements crop it, usually the most important words.
6. **Copied from a competitor.** Ad platforms group visually similar creative and suppress distinct delivery, so you pay for reach you do not receive.
7. **Same speaker and framing across every variant.** The system reads it as one asset, and your test learns nothing.
8. **Wrong funnel stage.** A cold-traffic curiosity hook shown to people who already booked a call wastes both the impression and their patience.
9. **Trying to sell inside the hook.** It opens; the body sells. A hook that closes has no reason for anyone to keep watching.

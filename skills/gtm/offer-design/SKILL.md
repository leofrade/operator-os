# Offer Design

> A price objection is almost always a value-articulation failure that happened weeks before the call, in what you packaged rather than what you said.

## The question this answers

- Why do buyers keep comparing us line by line against a cheaper competitor?
- What goes into the package, and what do I strip out even though it took work to build?
- How much higher can I price before demand breaks, and what has to be true first?
- Is the offer weak, or is the market wrong? Those need different fixes.

## When to run it

Run this when the offer is the constraint: win rates hold but discounts creep, or prospects agree with the diagnosis and stall at the number, or your proposal could carry a competitor's logo and nobody would notice.

**Do not run this when** the constraint is volume rather than conversion. If you close a healthy share of the conversations you have and simply have too few of them, offer work is procrastination — go to `lead-engine`. Skip it too when fewer than ten real buying conversations sit behind you: you cannot design against friction you have not heard described in a customer's own words.

## The method

### Move 1 — Confirm the market can carry the price

Score the segment on four things before touching the package: the pain is expensive and recurring; the buyer controls or can requisition budget; a list, association, or platform concentrates them; the segment is flat or growing. Fail two of four and stop — that is a market problem wearing an offer costume.

Then narrow. The same intellectual property sold to "operations managers" and to "operations managers at multi-site industrial distributors handling hazardous freight" is not the same product commercially, even when the deliverable is byte-identical. Specificity buys pricing power because it removes the buyer's ability to imagine a generic substitute. Narrow until it feels uncomfortable, then one click more.

**Exit criterion:** you can name the segment in a sentence a stranger could use to find five qualified accounts.

### Move 2 — Inventory the friction, not the features

List every reason the buyer does not get the outcome today, sequenced across before, during, and after working with you. Push for twenty to forty entries. Thin lists produce thin offers: each entry is a component you can be paid for removing.

Sort each entry into one of four buckets. Two of them raise value when you improve them, two lower value when you fail to reduce them.

| Bucket | The buyer's internal sentence | Your lever |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome size | "Even if it works, it isn't worth much to me" | Reframe to a bigger, more countable result |
| Belief | "It works, but not for a company like mine" | Proof, specificity, similar-account evidence |
| Time to value | "Results are months away and I need this quarter" | Sequenced early wins, faster first milestone |
| Effort and risk | "This becomes my project on top of my job" | Do it for them; remove steps, not just document them |

Most teams over-invest in the first two. The last two are where the money is: buyers pay premiums to have time and effort removed far more reliably than they pay for a bigger promise, because a bigger promise also raises disbelief.

**Exit criterion:** every entry has a counter-move phrased as something you would show a buyer, not tell them.

### Move 3 — Trim before you stack

Score each counter-move on two axes: cost to deliver, and how far it moves one of the four buckets. Kill everything low-impact regardless of cost, including the expensive things you are proud of. What survives is a high-value core plus cheap, high-perceived-value assets: templates, audits, configured artifacts, access.

Then assemble. Each surviving component gets a benefit-stating name, a defensible standalone worth, and an explicit link to the friction it removes. The test of a real stack is not length — it is that every component maps to something a buyer said out loud. Components with no matching friction read as padding, which lowers trust in the whole package.

**Threshold:** defensible perceived value of three to ten times price. Below three, the buyer negotiates. Above ten, the buyer starts hunting for the catch.

### Move 4 — Set price from the value gap, not the market average

Price off value you can evidence, then check it against what the market tolerates — never the reverse. Averaging competitors calibrates you to the median firm in your category, usually one with weak margins and no capacity to reinvest.

Raise price only after the stack is built; the order matters. Meet the discount reflex by adding rather than subtracting. Every discount teaches this buyer that your number is fiction and trains the next one to wait. Additions preserve the reference price, and the reference price is what you renew against.

### Move 5 — Add pressure last, and only honest pressure

Constraints on quantity and time raise conversion, and both are trivially faked, which makes them the fastest way to lose a sophisticated buyer. Use only limits you would defend under audit: real onboarding capacity, real cohort dates, a real expiry you intend to enforce.

Risk reversal is the highest-leverage single edit available. Structure it as a commitment with a consequence: if a named result does not happen by a named date, a named remedy triggers. A guarantee without a remedy is decoration.

**B2B translation.** Consumer offers carry broad unconditional money-back guarantees because refund rates stay low and the buyer spends their own money. Enterprise offers should not. Use conditional structures — continued service at no charge, a milestone-gated second payment, an early exit window, a fee at risk against a defined result. They are stronger commercially and survive procurement and legal review, which unconditional refund language often does not. And in B2B the party you are de-risking is usually the champion rather than the company: their career exposure is the real risk, so write the clause so they can forward it to their boss unedited.

## Failure modes

**Stack as landfill** — the package grows past a dozen components because adding felt free, and the buyer now reads it as filler around a thin core. The tell is a proposal your own reps cannot summarize in three sentences. → Cut to the components buyers referenced unprompted in the last five deals. Length is not value; correspondence to stated friction is.

**Inflated value math** — you assign a standalone worth to each component, total it, and the number is absurd relative to the price. The tell is that buyers stop reading at the total. → Only assign worth you could defend with a real market comparable, and stop stacking once the multiple is credible.

**Guarantee as adverse selection** — a strong guarantee attracts the buyers least likely to succeed, because they are buying the exit rather than the outcome. The tell is refunds and cancellations clustering in one segment or one acquisition channel. → Condition the guarantee on the client actions that actually drive results. Conditions are a filter, and they improve delivered results and reputation at once.

**Discount by another name** — the team stops cutting price and starts adding bonuses that cost real delivery hours. The tell is gross margin drifting down while list price holds. → Cap the cost-to-deliver of anything given away and track it beside discount.

**Fake scarcity** — a limit is announced, the deadline passes, the offer is still there. The tell is one buyer asking whether the deadline is real. → Use only constraints you enforce. Stating true capacity converts nearly as well and costs nothing when tested.

**Rebuilding the engine instead of the wrapper** — response softens and the team rewrites the whole offer rather than the presentation. The tell is a new package every quarter and no compounding learning. → Change in ascending order of cost: creative, wording, name, term length, then components. Rebuild the offer only when the underlying economics moved.

## Outputs

- A one-page segment definition with the four-criteria score and narrowing rationale.
- A friction inventory sorted into the four buckets, one counter-move per entry.
- The trimmed component list: names, defensible standalone worth, friction removed.
- A price recommendation showing the value gap, plus payment structure.
- A risk-reversal clause in "if not X by Y, then Z" form, with its conditions.
- The one constraint you will enforce, and who enforces it.

## Lineage

The commercial logic descends from Alex Hormozi's *$100M Offers* — in particular the insight that perceived value is a ratio with time and effort in the denominator, and that packaging is a pricing instrument rather than a marketing one. The market-selection discipline owes a debt to the direct-response tradition Hormozi draws on, Dan Kennedy especially, on the strategic worthlessness of being second-cheapest.

This articulation departs from the source twice. It demotes the enhancement layer — scarcity, urgency, bonuses — to the last and least important move, because in complex sales those levers decay fast and damage trust when overused. And it inverts the guarantee guidance above consumer price points, where conditional structures beat unconditional ones commercially and legally.

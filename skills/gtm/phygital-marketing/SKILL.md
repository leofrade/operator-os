# Phygital Marketing

> Customers stopped treating physical and digital as separate places; your budgets and your data did not, and that seam is what they feel.

## The question this answers

- Which touchpoints should we automate, which augment, and which defend from automation?
- A vendor has quoted us an immersive build. Over what horizon does it pay back, and what gets cut to fund it?
- We bought the technology and engagement did not move. What did we get wrong?
- Our physical and digital systems do not share a customer record. Do we fix that first, or layer experience on top of it?

## When to run it

Run this when you operate physical touchpoints — stores, branches, sites, service visits, events — alongside digital channels and the two behave like strangers. Run it also when a technology decision is being made off a vendor demonstration, or when a competitor's press release has created internal pressure to respond.

**Do not run this when** you are pre-product-market-fit or purely digital. The subject here is the seam between two modes of contact; with only one, it optimizes nothing. Skip it too when fundamentals are broken: if fulfillment is unreliable, inventory data is wrong, or support does not resolve, every experience layer you add is a more expensive way to disappoint people.

## The method

### Move 1 — Pay the integration debt before buying anything new

The entry gate, and the move most programs skip because it is unglamorous. Three walkthroughs, run by someone who did not design the system. Start a purchase in one channel and finish in another: does the customer repeat information they already gave? Contact support after a physical interaction: does the agent see it? Ask a store or field employee what they can see about the customer in front of them, and compare it with what the website knows.

If any fails, the constraint is identity and data continuity, not experience design. No immersive layer compensates for a company that forgets the customer between rooms; it makes the forgetting more conspicuous.

**Exit criterion:** all three pass, or remediation is funded and scheduled before any experience spend is approved.

### Move 2 — Classify every touchpoint by why a human is standing there

Not every human touchpoint has the same economics. Four roles, with sharply different correct treatments.

| The human is there to | Treatment | Signal you got it right |
|---|---|---|
| Process a transaction | Automate fully | Throughput up, satisfaction flat or up, staff redeployed rather than cut |
| Close a trust gap on a high-consideration purchase | Augment: digital for exploration, human for commitment | Time to decision falls, conversion holds |
| Supply expertise that is part of the product | Hybrid: digital handles the routine, human handles judgment | Expert time per customer rises while cost per customer falls |
| Be the product | Protect: automate the administration around them | Staff time in front of customers rises measurably |

The expensive mistake is treating the fourth category as the first. Automating a touchpoint whose entire value was the human interaction produces a cost saving in one quarter and a churn problem two years later, by which point nobody connects the two.

**Exit criterion:** every touchpoint on the journey map carries one of the four labels, agreed with the people who staff it.

### Move 3 — Instrument only where data changes a decision

Sensors, beacons, computer vision, and behavioral capture are cheap to install and expensive to justify. Before any of it, name the decision the data will change and who makes it. If the answer is "we will understand our customers better," you are buying a dashboard nobody opens.

Two hard constraints. Anything captured in a physical space must be disclosed plainly and refusable without penalty; unannounced tracking, once discovered, costs more than the personalization earned. And a recommendation revealing inference the customer never volunteered reads as surveillance — tolerance for precision is far lower in a physical space, because the customer never experienced themselves as logging in.

**Exit criterion:** each capture maps to a named decision, a named owner, and a disclosure the customer can act on.

### Move 4 — Design the experience against outcomes, not against the checklist

Five elements make an experience land: it engages more than one sense; it responds rather than broadcasts; it invites participation; it removes friction from peripheral steps like payment, access, and waiting; and it holds together as one story rather than a set of installations.

Use them as a diagnostic, not a shopping list. Take one journey and one metric — conversion, dwell, repeat rate, attachment — ask which element is most absent, and fix that one. Pursuing all five at once produces expensive, incoherent showrooms that win design awards and move nothing. Friction removal usually comes first: removing a queue, an authentication step, or a payment interruption beats adding a sensory layer and is measurable within a quarter.

**Exit criterion:** one element chosen, one metric named, one baseline recorded before anything is built.

### Move 5 — Sequence technology by payback, and pilot with a kill criterion

Rough order of return: frictionless payment and access, then presence-based contextual triggering, then augmented visualization where the product is hard to imagine in place, then anything persistently virtual.

Fully virtual worlds sit last for a reason. A generation of brand investment there was written off with almost nothing recovered, because audiences were smaller than reported and the experiences gave nobody a reason to return. The defensible version is participation where an audience already gathers for its own reasons, at a budget you can lose without a post-mortem.

Every pilot gets three things before it starts: a success threshold, an end date, and a named person authorized to stop it. Pilots without kill criteria become infrastructure by default.

**Exit criterion:** a sequenced roadmap with payback estimates and a signed kill criterion per pilot.

### Move 6 — Segment by behavior, not by birth year

Generational labels are the weakest available segmentation and the most used, because they are free and sound like insight. The behaviors that matter — checking a price on a phone in the aisle, continuing an in-person conversation in an app, expecting purchase history at every touchpoint — are near-universal across age groups, varying by category and comfort rather than decade of birth.

Segment on observed behavior instead: channel-switching frequency, self-service preference, response to presence-based prompts. Where a generational read is load-bearing it shows up there; where it does not, it was decoration.

**Exit criterion:** segments defined by observed behavior, with generational labels absent.

## Failure modes

**Technology-led strategy** — a vendor demonstration becomes a roadmap item. The tell is a project named after a technology rather than a customer problem. → Require the problem statement and baseline metric before the vendor is invited back.

**Immersion theater** — an installation that photographs beautifully and moves no number. The tell is a results deck leading with visitor counts and press coverage. → Declare the metric before the build and report it whether it moves or not.

**Automating the product** — the human interaction customers were paying for is replaced with a kiosk. The tell is cost per transaction improving while satisfaction slides for three quarters. → Apply the Move 2 classification honestly, and give the fourth category a veto.

**Personalization creep** — a recommendation demonstrates knowledge the customer never volunteered. The tell is complaints calling the experience unsettling rather than wrong. → Personalize on declared data first, and never make inference visible in a physical space.

**Metaverse FOMO** — a virtual presence built because a competitor announced one. The tell is nobody able to say who the audience is. → Fund it as a capped experiment, or say no in one sentence.

**Pilot immortality** — an initiative that neither scales nor dies, consuming budget permanently. → No pilot starts without a kill criterion and a named decision owner.

**Layering on broken fundamentals** — experience spend rising while complaint volume holds steady, because delivery or inventory data is the real problem. → Fix the base. A better-designed disappointment is still a disappointment.

## Outputs

- A three-walkthrough integration audit with pass or fail per test and a remediation plan.
- A journey map with every touchpoint classified into the four human-role categories.
- A data capture register: each signal, the decision it informs, its owner, its disclosure.
- One prioritized intervention with the chosen element, target metric, and baseline.
- A technology roadmap sequenced by payback, with virtual-world investment positioned.
- Pilot charters with success thresholds, end dates, and named kill authority.
- Behavior-based segment definitions.

## Lineage

Philip Kotler, Hermawan Kartajaya, and Iwan Setiawan's *Marketing 6.0* named this convergence and supplied its most durable contributions: the layered model of enabling technologies, environments, and experiences; the observation that a human touchpoint plays different economic roles in different purchases; and the elements that separate an immersive experience from a decorated one.

This articulation departs on sequence and skepticism. Where the source presents technology adoption as a maturity ladder to climb, this treats it as capital allocation with a payback order, in which the least glamorous item — data continuity across channels — comes first and takes most of the budget. It declines the generational framing that anchors the source's audience chapter, and places persistent virtual environments last rather than at the frontier, on the evidence of what that category returned to the operators who funded it.

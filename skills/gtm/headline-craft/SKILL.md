# Headline Craft

> A headline does not sell anything; it makes one specific person decide the next sentence is worth their time, and every technique that wins the click while breaking that promise costs more than it returns.

## The question this answers

- Why does this page get read and nothing happens after the first paragraph?
- We have twelve headline options and no way to choose between them that isn't taste.
- Which of these psychological triggers will make a serious buyer trust us less?
- What do I test first, and how long before the result means anything?

## When to run it

Run this whenever the first line carries the weight: paid creative, cold email subject lines, landing page titles, deck section headings, proposal titles. Run it as a diagnostic too when a page has traffic and no conversion — if people arrive and leave without scrolling, the headline made a promise the page does not keep, and rewriting the body will not fix that.

**Do not run this when** you cannot yet state what the product does for whom. Headline work on an undefined offer produces clever sentences that test well and sell nothing. Go to [`gtm/offer-design`](../../gtm/offer-design/SKILL.md) first. Do not run it either on a page with fewer than a few hundred qualified visits a month; you will never get a readable test, and the rubric in Move 5 beats an underpowered experiment.

## The method

### Move 1 — Write the promise before the headline

One sentence, three parts: who it is for, the measurable change, the constraint that no longer applies.

```
For <specific role in specific context>,
<measurable change>
without <the cost, effort, or risk they assume is required>.
```

If you cannot fill the third slot, you have a feature description. The removed constraint carries nearly all the energy, and it is the part most writers skip, because it requires knowing what the buyer believes the outcome costs.

**Exit criterion:** someone who has never seen the product can restate the promise after one reading.

### Move 2 — Locate the reader's awareness, then choose what the headline leads with

Where the reader stands determines what the first line may say. Leading with the offer to someone who does not believe they have the problem reads as noise; leading with problem education to someone already comparing vendors wastes their time.

| Reader state | Headline leads with | It fails when it |
|---|---|---|
| Does not know the problem exists | A consequence they recognize, or a specific scene | Names your category |
| Feels the pain, sees no solution | The problem, sharply, with its real cost | Jumps to the product |
| Comparing approaches | The mechanism — why your path works differently | Claims to be "better" |
| Comparing vendors | Proof, specificity, direct comparison | Restates a generic benefit |
| Ready to act | The offer and its terms | Adds persuasion they no longer need |

Read the market too. If your ten nearest competitors promise variations of one outcome, an outcome headline is invisible however well written, and the only moves left are mechanism and identity.

**Exit criterion:** one row circled, defensible with something a customer said.

### Move 3 — Inventory what you can actually substantiate

List every number, source, credential, and named customer you are permitted to use. Nothing enters a headline that is not on the list.

This is the highest-leverage constraint in the method. Specificity is the most reliable amplifier in the discipline — "cuts monthly close from eleven days to four" beats "dramatically accelerates your close" by margins that dwarf every trigger choice — but invented specificity is worse than vagueness, because it is discoverable and it is what a procurement team screenshots.

**Exit criterion:** every claim maps to a source you would show a lawyer.

### Move 4 — Generate a forced spread across four families

Do not generate twenty variants of the family you naturally write in. Write three in each of four families, so the spread tests hypotheses rather than adjectives.

| Family | The mechanism | Highest risk |
|---|---|---|
| Specificity | A number or scene so precise it implies first-hand knowledge | None, if the number is real |
| Loss | Naming what the status quo is costing right now | Fear that reads as manipulation |
| Mechanism | A named, explainable reason this works when other things did not | Jargon dressed up as a method |
| Proof | Peers, scale, or an outcome someone else already got | Cherry-picking that a buyer can check |

Twelve candidates. Curiosity is not a family here but a modifier available inside any of them, and it is the most abused device in the discipline: it wins the metric the marketer reports while damaging the one the business runs on.

**Exit criterion:** twelve headlines, three per family, none a rephrasing of another.

### Move 5 — Score before you test

Every candidate passes or fails five checks. Ties break downward, toward the plainer option.

```
1. Substantiated   — every claim traces to Move 3's list
2. Self-selecting  — the wrong reader can tell it is not for them
3. Kept            — the next screen delivers exactly what was promised
4. Specific        — contains a concrete noun, number, or scene
5. Sayable         — you would say it out loud to the buyer, in a room, unembarrassed
```

Check 5 does most of the work in B2B. A headline a rep would never speak to a CFO's face tells that CFO you are marketing at them. This is where the direct-response inheritance gets filtered: secrecy framing, guru revelation, "one weird trick," fabricated countdowns, and income-style claims all raise response in consumer impulse categories and all lower trust in a purchase the reader will be blamed for internally.

**Exit criterion:** two or three survivors, and a written reason each other candidate died.

### Move 6 — Test on the metric that pays

Test one variable at a time. Run to a pre-declared sample, not to the moment the chart looks good; without a few hundred conversions per arm you are reading noise and shipping superstition.

Judge on the downstream number — qualified conversion, reply rate, booked meeting — never clicks alone. A headline that raises click-through and lowers qualified conversion has bought unqualified attention with your credibility.

**Exit criterion:** a winner, the downstream delta, and the losers filed with what they disproved.

## Failure modes

**Trigger stacking** — authority, scarcity, secrecy, and a number crammed into one line. The tell is a headline that reads as three welded together. → One mechanism per headline. Everything else moves to the subhead or dies.

**Curiosity without payoff** — the click is won by concealment and the page opens with something ordinary. The tell is high click-through and a bounce rate above 80 percent. → Move the interesting thing to the top of the page. If there is nothing interesting, the offer is the problem.

**The template tell** — "Finally, a 100 percent proven way to X without Y." Practitioners in the category have seen the construction ten thousand times and read it as a signal of who wrote it. → Keep the underlying structure, rewrite in the buyer's own vocabulary, and delete every intensifier.

**Invented specificity** — a plausible-looking number nobody can source. The tell is nobody in the room knowing where it came from. → Cut it, or measure it. A softer honest claim survives a procurement review; a hard fake one ends the deal.

**Optimizing the wrong number** — variant B wins on click-through; sales notices lead quality dropping a month later. → Declare the downstream metric before the test starts, and hold it.

**Consumer tactics in a committee sale** — fake deadlines, artificial scarcity, and "secrets the industry doesn't want you to know" pointed at an enterprise buyer. The tell is your champion declining to forward the page internally. → If a champion cannot forward it without embarrassment, it does not ship. That single test removes most of what does not transfer.

**Headline written last** — the page gets built and the title bolted on at the end. The tell is a headline that summarizes rather than promises. → Write it first and let it discipline the page.

## Outputs

- The promise sentence in the Move 1 template.
- A stated reader-awareness position with the market-crowding read behind it.
- A substantiation list: every usable number, source, and named reference.
- Twelve candidates, three per family, labeled by family.
- A scored rubric table with two or three survivors and a kill reason for the rest.
- A test plan: one variable, pre-declared sample size, declared downstream metric.

## Lineage

This descends from the measured direct-response tradition: John Caples, who established that headlines are testable and that specificity and self-interest beat cleverness by measurable margins; David Ogilvy, on the discipline of the promise and respect for the reader; Eugene Schwartz, whose account of awareness and market sophistication supplies Move 2 almost entirely; and Joseph Sugarman, on each line's only job being to earn the next.

The departure is deliberate. That tradition optimizes for response in impulse categories; this version subordinates response to credibility. Where those authors would test any device that lifts click-through, this method disqualifies one a champion could not forward inside their own company. The trade costs short-term response and is correct in any sale a person has to defend to their boss.

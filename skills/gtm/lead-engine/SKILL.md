# Lead Engine

> Most acquisition problems are one channel run at a quarter of the volume needed to produce a signal, abandoned before it could produce one.

## The question this answers

- We need pipeline this quarter. Which single channel gets the people and the money?
- How long must a channel run before I am allowed to conclude it does not work?
- Lead volume is up and revenue is flat. Where did that break?
- What do we give away to start a conversation, without attracting people who will never buy?

## When to run it

Run this when you convert the conversations you get but do not get enough of them, and you can name the buyer specifically enough to find them on purpose.

**Do not run this when** your close rate on qualified conversations is under roughly one in five. More leads into a leaky offer produces more losses, faster, and burns the channel while you learn nothing — fix `offer-design` first. Do not run it as a rescue when a large account is about to churn; acquisition does not move fast enough to solve a retention emergency.

## The method

### Move 1 — Choose one channel from two axes

Every acquisition method sits on two axes: whether the audience already knows you, and whether you address one person or many. That gives four options — warm outreach, published content, cold outreach, paid placement — differing in cost, latency, and how well they survive your absence.

| Channel | Time to first signal | Cash cost | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm outreach | Days | Near zero | Finite list; exhausts in weeks |
| Published content | 3–9 months | Time | Compounds slowly, dies fast if abandoned |
| Cold outreach | 3–6 weeks | Low, labor-heavy | List quality collapses at scale |
| Paid placement | 1–4 weeks | High, up front | Punishes weak conversion economics |

Pick one. Running all four at partial intensity produces four inconclusive experiments. Sequence instead: warm first, because it is free and it teaches you the objection set, then whichever of the other three matches your price point and cycle length.

**Decision rule:** low contract value with a self-serving buyer favors content and paid. High contract value with a named individual inside a known account list favors cold outreach with a research step. Under twenty target accounts, none of this applies — that is direct selling, not acquisition.

### Move 2 — Set the volume floor before you start

A channel is not tested until it has run at a volume that could produce a signal. Commit to a daily quantum and hold it for a fixed window before evaluating: a hundred outreach touches per day, or a hundred minutes of content production, or a defined daily spend, for six to eight weeks minimum.

Two commitment styles work. Fix the input — a hundred actions daily regardless of outcome — which protects against quiet abandonment. Or fix the output — work until three qualified meetings are booked — which forces creativity when a script dies. Input commitment while learning the channel, output commitment once you know its rates.

**Exit criterion:** you can state stage-by-stage conversion with at least thirty observations at the top. Below thirty, you have anecdotes.

### Move 3 — Build bait that filters, not bait that attracts

The purpose of a free asset is a conversation with someone who has the problem, the budget, and the authority. Download volume is a distraction. Name one narrow, expensive, specific problem — narrower than feels commercially sensible — solve it completely, make the next step obvious.

Choose format by what it signals about whoever consumes it. A generic guide attracts learners. A diagnostic, an audit, a calculator, or a working tool attracts operators, because engaging requires them to reveal their situation and spend effort only someone with the real problem would spend.

**Threshold:** if fewer than one in five people who see the offer take it, the asset or the promise is wrong. If more than half take it and almost none convert to a sales conversation, the bait attracts the wrong population — worse than low volume, because it corrupts what your team believes a lead is.

### Move 4 — Instrument the economics before you scale

Two numbers govern scalability: lifetime gross profit per customer, and fully loaded cost to acquire one. Track the ratio, and how long the cash takes to come back.

| Reading | Interpretation | Move |
|---|---|---|
| Ratio below 3:1 | Not scalable yet | Fix conversion or margin before spending more |
| 3:1 or better, payback beyond 90 days | Capped by cash, not demand | Restructure terms or add an early upsell |
| 3:1 or better, payback 30 to 90 days | Scalable at a financed pace — where most B2B lands | Grow spend only as fast as cash returns; buy the gap with annual prepay, a deposit, or a credit line before raising budget |
| 3:1 or better, payback inside 30 days | Scalable | Spend to the limit of delivery capacity |
| Above 10:1 | Under-invested | Spend more; volume is being left on the table |

Include labor, not just media. A channel that looks efficient on ad spend while consuming two full-time people is often the most expensive one you run. Expect to lose money first: budget roughly double what a new customer pays initially, and treat it as tuition.

### Move 5 — Add leverage only after the channel works

Once a channel produces predictable output at an acceptable ratio, multiply it through other people: customers who refer, employees who run the play, partners who resell. Each amplifies what exists; none repairs what does not work.

**B2B translation.** Most acquisition advice is written for consumer and information products, and four mechanics change. Referrals stop being spontaneous and become a structured ask — B2B buyers refer inside their function, the ask must be specific ("who else runs this process at a company your size?"), and it is timed to a delivered result, not to a payment. Affiliate programs do not translate; the analog is an implementation or channel partner whose economics depend on your product working, which needs enablement and margin, not a commission link. Paid placement rarely creates demand at enterprise price points — it captures existing intent and builds the recognition that makes cold outreach land, so judge it by outbound reply rates and branded search, not last-click attribution. And the lead is a buying group, not a person: one engaged contact is a signal, not an opportunity, until a second appears from a different function.

## Failure modes

**The magnet that trains the wrong buyer** — a broad, popular free asset generates volume and a pipeline of people who will never have budget. The tell is rising lead count with a falling meeting-to-opportunity rate. → Rebuild it around a problem only your buyer has, and accept a lower opt-in rate as the price of a clean pipeline.

**Channel roulette** — three weeks of outbound, then content, then paid, because none "worked." The tell is that nobody can state a conversion rate from memory. → Commit to one channel for a full window at the volume floor, and write the quit criteria before starting so the decision is not made on a bad Tuesday.

**Scaling a broken conversion motion** — the channel produces meetings, meetings do not close, the response is more spend. The tell is cost per acquisition climbing while cost per meeting holds flat. → That is an offer or sales problem. Cap spend and fix the conversation, not the traffic.

**Automation before message-market fit** — sequences go to thousands of contacts before one version has earned replies by hand. The tell is a reply rate under one percent and a sender domain in trouble. → Automate only a message you have already watched work manually.

**Vanity audience** — followers grow, pipeline does not, because the content is optimized for the platform rather than the buyer. The tell is engagement from peers and competitors, none from customers. → Measure content by conversations started, and make a direct ask periodically; an audience never told what you sell will not buy it.

**Permanent outsourcing** — an agency runs the channel indefinitely and the knowledge lives outside the company. The tell is that nobody internal can explain why performance moved last month. → Buy an agency engagement to learn a channel on a fixed term with a defined handover.

## Outputs

- A single channel choice with rationale and rejected alternatives.
- A volume floor: daily quantum, window length, stop/continue criteria written in advance.
- A lead magnet spec: narrow problem, format, the filter it applies, opt-in threshold.
- An economics sheet: lifetime gross profit, loaded acquisition cost, ratio, payback period.
- Message and sequence drafts at the volume the floor requires.
- A leverage plan naming which multiplier gets added, and at what trigger.

## Lineage

The channel taxonomy and the volume discipline come from Alex Hormozi's *$100M Leads*, whose central and correct claim is that most companies have an acquisition volume problem rather than an acquisition strategy problem. The efficiency ratio and the emphasis on recovering acquisition cost quickly are his too, and are the part most often skipped.

This version departs from the source by constraining channel choice with sales cycle and contract value rather than founder preference, and by weighting filtering over volume in lead magnet design. In enterprise sales, a free asset that attracts ten thousand of the wrong people is a liability, not a slow start.

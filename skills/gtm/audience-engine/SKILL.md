# Audience Engine

> An audience you own is the only distribution asset that appreciates; every other channel is rented, and the landlord reprices without asking.

## The question this answers

- What angle can we own that nobody else is publishing, and how would we prove it before spending a year on it?
- We publish consistently and nothing compounds. Is the problem the writing, the distribution, or the angle?
- When do we stop building the audience and start selling to it?
- Should we add a second channel now, or is that exactly how the first one dies?

## When to run it

Run this when distribution is your constraint and you can fund an eighteen-month payback: your product is discoverable but not discovered, your paid channels work only while the card is being charged, or your category has enough shared confusion that being the clearest voice in it is worth something.

**Do not run this when** your addressable market is a few hundred named accounts you could list in a spreadsheet. Publishing to attract two hundred buyers is a slow way to do what an outbound sequence and three conferences do in a quarter — go to `lead-engine` instead. Also skip it when you need revenue inside two quarters. This engine has a nine-to-eighteen-month ignition period and no honest way to compress it. Starting it under revenue pressure guarantees you abandon it at month five, having paid the full cost and collected none of the return.

## The method

### Move 1 — Fix the angle before you touch a calendar

The angle is three things multiplied: a subject where you have unfair operating knowledge, a stance nobody else takes on it, and an audience narrow enough to name individuals in.

The test is subtractive. Name the three sources your intended reader uses today for this subject. Now say, in one sentence, what all three systematically get wrong. If you cannot, you have a topic, not an angle, and topics do not compound — the incumbent's back catalog outranks you forever.

Write the mission in this shape, and refuse to publish until it holds:

```
<channel> is where <specific audience>
gets <specific material>
so they can <specific outcome>.
```

No mention of what you sell. If your product appears in that sentence, the angle is an advertisement wearing a costume.

**Exit criterion:** you can name ten real people who fit the audience, and predict which two of them would argue with your stance.

### Move 2 — One channel, one format, twenty-six units before judgment

Pick the channel you can sustain in your worst month, not your best. Everything else is amplification.

| Surface | Control | Reach at zero | Use it as |
|---|---|---|---|
| Newsletter, owned site | Total | None; you build it | The asset |
| Long video, podcast | Partial | High | The discovery engine |
| Social platforms | None | High, decaying | The billboard pointing home |

The failure is not choosing wrong, it is choosing three. Publish twenty-six consecutive units on one surface before you evaluate anything. Below that sample you are reading noise, and every early pivot resets the clock.

**Exit criterion:** a publishing cadence that survived a bad month without a gap.

### Move 3 — Convert readers into subscribers you own

Count one number: subscribers on a list you could export today. Impressions, followers, and view counts are inputs at best and self-deception at worst.

Health thresholds on that list: open rate above 35 percent, reply rate above 1 percent, and unsubscribes below 0.5 percent per send. A list that grows while opens fall is not an audience — it is a directory of people who once clicked something.

**Exit criterion:** subscriber growth is reported weekly and no other content metric appears in a leadership deck.

### Move 4 — Engineer ignition before you engineer volume

Publishing is not distribution. In a saturated category, unignited content has approximately zero half-life, and cold followers convert to action at rates indistinguishable from zero.

So build the ignition list deliberately: thirty to a hundred named people who already care, cultivated by hand. Reply to every comment for the first ninety days. Send the piece to specific individuals with a specific reason it is relevant to them, before it goes wide. Track who actually moves.

Build shareability into the piece before publishing rather than promoting after. A piece is shareable when it carries a position someone can agree or disagree with publicly, one number or observation that is yours and cannot be sourced elsewhere, and a shape that makes the sharer look informed to their peers. Neutral, comprehensive, correct content is the most reliably ignored category on the internet.

**Threshold:** if under 20 percent of your ignition list engages within 48 hours, the piece was weak or the list was never real. Both are fixable; conflating them is not.

### Move 5 — Gate monetization on self-propulsion

The audience is ready to be sold to when it grows without you. Four consecutive weeks in which at least 40 percent of new subscribers arrive from sources that are neither paid nor you personally is a defensible gate. Before that gate, take revenue from things that do not consume audience trust: sponsorship in limited slots, advisory, paid workshops. After it, launch the product the audience has been describing to you in replies.

**Exit criterion:** a documented monetization decision with the growth evidence attached, not a vibe.

### Move 6 — Make the kill decision on schedule

At month nine, with the full sample in hand, decide once: continue, rebase the angle, or stop. Continue if subscriber growth is compounding month over month and reply quality is rising. Rebase if engagement is fine but growth is flat — that is an angle problem, not an effort problem. Stop if neither moved. Continuing by default is how content programs become permanent cost centers nobody is willing to cancel.

## Failure modes

**Encyclopedia mode** — you cover the category comprehensively, accurately, and neutrally, and nothing gets shared. The tell is high praise in private and zero amplification in public. → One piece, one argument, stated in the first two sentences. Anything you would not defend in a room is filler.

**Vanity dashboard** — the report leads with impressions and follower growth because subscriber growth is embarrassing. The tell is a metric change mid-quarter. → Freeze the primary metric for four quarters before you are allowed to argue with it.

**Premature diversification** — a second channel launches before the first compounds, and both starve. The tell is "we should be on video too" arriving in month four. → New surfaces are earned by the first surface hitting Move 5's gate, never by anxiety.

**Sales-speak drift** — pieces about your product, your funding, your release notes. Engagement drops hard and stays down. → Cap product content at one in ten pieces, and make that one genuinely useful to someone who will never buy.

**Borrowed-land build** — the entire audience lives inside a platform, and a ranking change removes 60 percent of reach in a week. The tell is no export path. → Every piece has one job beyond being read: move someone to a list you control.

**The ghost list** — subscriber count rises, opens fall, and nobody says it out loud. → Sunset non-openers at 180 days. A smaller list that reads you is an asset; a large one that does not is a bill.

**Ignition theater** — a share button, a "please repost," and a hope. The tell is the same twelve accounts amplifying everything. → Ignition is named people asked individually. If the list is not written down, it does not exist.

## Outputs

- A one-sentence angle statement plus the named gap in the three incumbent sources.
- The editorial mission in the template above, with the ten named readers.
- A channel decision with the sustainable cadence and the twenty-six-unit evaluation date.
- The subscriber dashboard: count, growth rate, open rate, reply rate, unsubscribe rate.
- A written ignition list of thirty to a hundred named people, with engagement tracked per piece.
- A pre-publish shareability check applied to each piece.
- A monetization gate decision with the four-week source-mix evidence.

## Lineage

Joe Pulizzi's *Content Inc.* argued the sequence that this skill preserves: build the audience first, learn what it needs from proximity to it, and monetize afterward. Mark Schaefer's *The Content Code* supplied the correction that makes it survivable now — that publication is not distribution, that content volume has outrun human attention, and that a small, cultivated group of engaged people determines whether anything travels.

This articulation departs from both in its treatment of the option itself. Pulizzi's framing implies the audience path is broadly right; here it is a conditional bet with an explicit disqualifier for concentrated B2B markets, a fixed evaluation window, and a scheduled kill decision. The intent is that the honest outcome of running this method is sometimes "do not run this method."

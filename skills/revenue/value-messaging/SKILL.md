# Value Messaging

> Whoever describes the customer's problem more precisely than the customer can owns the transaction — and the price.

## The question this answers

- Why does this buyer keep asking about price instead of outcomes?
- What do I say to a CFO that I cannot say to an end user, and vice versa?
- How do I make my strengths become the evaluation criteria instead of answering someone else's?
- What exactly does my champion say in the meeting I am not invited to?

## When to run it

Run it when the deal has an economic buyer, a budget that has to be defended
against other projects, and at least one credible alternative. Run it before a
pitch rewrite, before an executive meeting, and any time a deal stalls with
"we're still evaluating."

**Do not run this when** the product is bought on price and availability, the
buyer is a single individual spending their own budget, or you have not yet run
customer discovery. Messaging built before you have heard buyers describe the
problem in their own words is fiction with good grammar.

## The method

### 1. Write the current state in their words, not yours

Five bullets, one sentence each, describing how the buyer's world is broken
today. The constraint that matters: every word has to be a word they would use.
The moment vendor vocabulary appears, you have started pitching.

Test: read the five bullets to a customer and ask them to correct you. If they
correct nothing, you were generic. If they correct one and add two, you have
credibility and they have started doing your discovery for you.

### 2. Chain each item to currency, then stop

An acknowledged problem does not fund a project. A priced problem does. Ask "so
what?" until the answer is a number, and stop as soon as you have money plus a
strategic or personal consequence.

```
"Approvals are manual"            → so what?
"Three days lost per cycle"       → so what?
"Shipments miss their window"     → what does that cost?
"Penalties plus expedite fees"    → and at annual run rate?
"Low seven figures, and it is on the operations director's scorecard"
STOP.
```

Threshold: if the annual cost of the problem is not at least 3x the annual cost
of your solution, you do not have a business case, you have a preference. Either
find a larger instance of the problem or requalify the deal.

### 3. Write outcomes a CFO would move budget for

The test for a business outcome is brutal and singular: is this strong enough
that the finance sponsor pulls budget away from a competing internal project to
fund it? If the honest answer is "maybe," rewrite it.

| Rejected | Accepted |
|---|---|
| Greater agility | 32% reduction in unit processing cost within 12 months |
| Better scalability | Six months removed from the release cycle |
| Improved visibility | Downtime from 2.5% to under 0.3%, on a risk exposure they have quantified |
| Simplified integration | Two FTEs redeployed from reconciliation to planning |

### 4. Turn your advantages into their requirements

This is the move that decides competitive deals, and it happens before any
formal evaluation exists. Work backward from the outcome to the minimum set of
capabilities required to get there, written in the customer's technical language,
where two or three of them are things you do materially better than anyone else.

You do this by asking questions that force the buyer to price a gap, never by
naming a competitor. Attacking a competitor by name costs you advisor status
permanently and is the amateur move.

```
Weak:  "Careful with vendor X, their system runs in batches."
Right: "If recovery only begins after a three-hour batch window, what does
        that cost you in SLA credits and expedites on a bad day?"
```

The buyer quantifies the gap themselves. Real-time stops being a nice-to-have
and becomes an elimination criterion. Do the same to the two invisible
competitors: "how long did your last three internal builds take to reach the
maturity you need in six months?" and "if nothing changes for twelve months,
what does that metric look like when the board asks?"

### 5. Triage your differentiators — most of them are not

Sort every claim into three buckets and enforce the counts. Discipline here is
what stops a pitch from turning into a catalog.

| Type | How many | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Unique | 1–2 | Genuinely unavailable elsewhere today. Expires. Use surgically. |
| Comparative | 4–6 | Everyone has a version; yours is materially better. This is where deals are won. |
| Institutional | 1–2 | Funding, scale, support footprint. Reduces career risk for the buyer. |

Everything else is table stakes. Saying it out loud implies you think it is
special, which tells the buyer you are junior.

### 6. Pressure-test every claim twice

**So what?** — can this be converted into revenue gained, cost removed, or risk
priced? If there is no arithmetic, delete it from the deck.

**Says who?** — is it backed by an auditable case, third-party data, or a live
demonstration? "Market leader" and "studies show" are not evidence. A logo wall
without a story behind it is a confession.

### 7. Write the recap paragraph and open every meeting with it

One paragraph, assembled from the customer's own sentences: the problem, its
cost in currency, the capabilities required, and the outcome. Not an elevator
pitch — the opposite of one, because none of the words are yours.

```
"Last session, what we heard from you and the operations team is that
[problem] is costing roughly [amount] a year in [consequences]. Fixing it
requires [capability A], [capability B], and [capability C] — which would
get you to [outcome with a number]."
```

Open every subsequent meeting by reading it and asking for corrections. A
correction is the highest-value information in the deal: it means a stakeholder
changed, a priority moved, or the board reset the agenda. It also gives your
champion a paragraph they can paste into an internal email verbatim, which is
what internal selling actually looks like.

## Failure modes

**Feature dumping under pressure** — the buyer goes quiet and the rep fills the
silence with capabilities. Tell: the demo starts in the first fifteen minutes.
→ Silence is a question. Answer it with the recap paragraph, not the product.

**Adjective outcomes** — the business case is a list of improvements with no
denominators. Tell: nobody in the room can state what the project is worth.
→ Apply the budget-reallocation test to each line. Cut what fails.

**Vendor vocabulary** — your category words appear in the customer's problem
statement. Tell: the buyer nods but never repeats your phrasing back. → Rewrite
using only sentences you have heard them say. If you have none, you have not
done discovery.

**Attacking competitors by name** — feels decisive, reads as insecure, and
permanently converts you from advisor into vendor. Tell: your battlecard is a
list of their weaknesses. → Convert each weakness into a question that makes the
buyer price the gap.

**Unfalsifiable claims** — the deck asserts superiority with no source. Tell:
every proof point is a logo. → One auditable case per major claim, or the claim
comes out.

**Messaging written in a room with no sellers in it** — marketing produces a
narrative the field never uses. Tell: reps have their own decks. → Build it with
sellers, engineers, and customer success in the room, then test it against live
deals in the current pipeline, not hypotheticals.

**Recap skipped once, then never** — meetings become disconnected events and
scope creeps. Tell: the buyer asks for something in week six that has no
relationship to the original problem. → Reopen with the recap. Every time.

## Outputs

- A one-page value map: current state, priced consequences, required
  capabilities, outcomes with numbers, proof points
- A recap paragraph in the customer's language, ready for a champion to forward
- A differentiator triage: 1–2 unique, 4–6 comparative, 1–2 institutional, with
  everything else explicitly marked table stakes
- Question sets that force the buyer to price the gap — including against doing
  nothing and building internally
- A rewritten pitch where every claim survives "so what?" and "says who?"

## Lineage

The method descends from Force Management's Command of the Message and its Value
Messaging Framework, which gave B2B sales its most durable insight: that value
is an architecture built jointly by sales, product, and marketing, not a slide
written by one of them. Where this articulation departs: the original is designed
as an organization-wide change program with a formal matrix and a certification
path, and much of that scaffolding only earns its cost above a hundred sellers.
What survives at any size is smaller and harder — customer words, a number, a
short list of capabilities you can defend, and one paragraph you are willing to
open every meeting with.

# Channel Strategy

> A channel is not distribution. It is an economy you are asking someone else to invest in, and it only works if they make money before you do.

## The question this answers

- Direct, through partners, or both — and what does hybrid actually cost?
- Why did we sign forty partners and get revenue from four?
- How do I stop my direct team and my partners fighting over the same account?
- Are we growing, or moving inventory into someone else's warehouse?

## When to run it

Run it when direct sales works but will not reach the geography, segment, or
implementation capacity you need, and your unit economics can absorb another
margin layer. Run it when an existing program has produced signups but no revenue.

**Do not run this when** you have not proven you can sell the product yourself.
Partners do not discover your value proposition; they resell an argument that
already works, and building a channel before product-market fit exports your
discovery problem to people with no incentive to solve it. Also skip it when
gross margin cannot fund both a partner margin and a program team — a channel is
not free distribution, it is a business unit.

## The method

### 1. Choose the model on economics, not on ambition

Three models, and the honest cost of each:

| Model | Buy it when | Real cost |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | High ticket, complex sale, you need the relationship and the data | Your own sales capacity, geography by geography |
| Indirect | You need reach, local presence, or implementation capacity you cannot hire | A permanent margin layer plus a program team; loss of direct end-customer contact |
| Hybrid | Segments genuinely differ — enterprise direct, long tail through partners | All of the above, plus the highest conflict risk in the business |

Hybrid is where most companies land and where most programs die. If you choose
it, the segmentation rule has to be written down, published, and obeyed by your
own direct team first. Partners forgive thin margins. They do not forgive being
outbid by the vendor on a deal they sourced.

### 2. Answer the only two questions a partner is asking

Every partner conversation reduces to: *how much will I make?* and *what support
do I get?* Program material should answer one of the two in its first paragraph.

Margin has to survive their cost of sale. Run the arithmetic from their side:
fully loaded cost per selling hour, the length of your sales cycle, deals a rep
can carry. If their margin does not cover the hours your product demands, you
have designed a program that rationally deserves to be ignored, and it will be.

### 3. Write commercial rules you are willing to be bound by

The program is a contract about behavior, not a benefits brochure. The minimum
worth publishing:

- **Deal registration** — who is protected on which opportunity, for how long
- **Margin and rebates** — including whether the vendor may ever sell below
  partner price
- **Co-marketing funds** — what qualifies, what proof is required, who approves
- **Post-sale responsibility** — who supports the customer, what the vendor covers
- **Tier movement** — how a partner is promoted or reclassified, with notice

The credibility test is not whether the rules are generous. It is whether the
vendor follows them the first time following them is expensive. One unhonored
registration destroys more trust than a year of enablement builds.

### 4. Make deal registration the trust primitive

This is what the whole program stands on. Register by opportunity, not by account
— an account-level lock rewards squatting. Require the end customer, contact,
scope, and expected date. Set a validity window tied to your real sales cycle and
expire it if the partner stops updating. First to register, following the rules,
gets the best commercial condition.

Add a back-end rebate for the partner who did the pre-sales work and lost the
transaction. Without it, partners stop investing in discovery and the program
degrades into price shopping.

### 5. Tier for behavior you want, and let partners move

Across the partner programs I have built or audited, the distribution settles near
5% top, 15% middle, 80% entry. That is an observed rule of thumb, not a law — it is
not drawn from published research, and it moves with deal size, partner type, and
how hard your certification is. Use it to sanity-check your first tier design, then
replace it with your own measured distribution by the end of the second period. If
your top tier holds a quarter of partners, the bar is too low to mean anything. Two
common mistakes: tiering purely on revenue, which rewards partners who inherited a
customer base over those building capability, and tiering purely on
certifications, which produces well-trained partners who sell nothing. Use both.

Movement rules matter more than the tiers. Promote after two consecutive periods
above the line. Do not downgrade anyone in their first year — they are still
ramping — and after that, reclassify only after two consecutive periods below.
New partners always enter at the base tier: the tier reflects your relationship,
not their résumé.

Keep the cut line distinct from the target. The cut line is common to the tier;
the target is individual, negotiated, and harder.

### 6. Manage sell-through, never sell-in

Sell-in is product entering a partner's inventory or pipeline. Sell-through is
product reaching the end customer. Managing only sell-in produces the classic
failure: an excellent quarter, followed by channel inventory, price erosion,
credit exposure, and two quarters where the partner buys nothing.

Compensate channel managers on both. Instrument sell-through even when it is hard
to see — everything you cannot measure at the end customer is a number you have
chosen to be surprised by.

### 7. Build joint business plans for the few, not the many

A written plan — targets, portfolio, pipeline contribution, agreed investments,
named owners with dates — is worth building with your top tier and nobody else.
Below that it will not be maintained by either side, and an unmaintained plan is
worse than none because it creates the impression of a managed relationship.

The two causes of failure are symmetric, and both are lack of commitment: the
partner's, or your own channel manager's. Assume the second one first.

## Failure modes

**Sell-in vanity** — the quarter is made by loading partners; the next two are
dead. Tell: partner inventory or unclosed registered pipeline grows faster than
end-customer revenue. → Report sell-through as the headline number. Pay on it.

**Unpoliced channel conflict** — the direct team closes deals partners sourced,
usually at quarter end. Tell: partners stop registering opportunities and start
asking about roadmap instead of deals. → Publish the segmentation rule and pay
the direct rep on partner-sourced deals in their territory, so engaging a partner
is never a personal financial loss.

**Co-marketing funds as margin repair** — funds reimburse partners for
discounting. Tell: claims arrive with no campaign attached. → Require a plan
approved in advance, a measurable objective, shared investment, and proof of
spend. A fund with no accountability is a rebate with extra paperwork.

**Recruiting partners who cannot sell value** — signing anyone who applies, then
wondering why they compete only on price. Tell: high partner count, revenue
concentrated in a handful. → Pre-qualify on customer base, technical capability,
and whether their business model rewards consultative selling. Ten committed
partners beat a hundred logos on a slide.

**Rules the vendor breaks first** — exceptions get granted whenever a large deal
is at stake. Tell: partners ask "is that the real rule or the written rule?"
→ Every exception is a precedent. Decide in advance which rules are absolute and
accept the revenue cost of holding them.

**Over-investing in the base tier** — program resources spread evenly across
hundreds of low producers. Tell: the channel team is busy and revenue
concentration never changes. → Serve the base tier with self-service. Spend human
capacity on the top two tiers and on recruiting partners who could reach them.

**Delegating market development** — the vendor hands the market to distributors
and disappears. Tell: nobody at the vendor has spoken to an end customer in six
months. → Market development is not transferable. Keep contact with every layer,
including end customers, and make clear in joint visits that you are supporting
the partner, not competing with them.

## Outputs

- A direct/indirect/hybrid recommendation with margin math and the segmentation
  rule that prevents conflict
- A commercial policy: registration, margins, co-marketing, post-sale
  responsibility, tier movement
- A tier model with cut lines, movement rules, and the benefit/commitment balance
- A partner pre-qualification checklist: customer base, capability, portfolio,
  business model
- A sell-in versus sell-through reporting design, plus the compensation change
  that makes it real
- Joint business plans for top-tier partners, with owners, dates, and investments

## Lineage

The structural material comes from Pedro Luiz Roccato's *O Grande Livro de Canais
de Vendas*, the most complete practitioner treatment of channel programs in the
Latin American market — the chain of producer, distributor, reseller and end
customer; deal registration as the foundation of partner trust; tiering by
revenue and by competence; the sell-in versus sell-through distinction; and the
insistence that market development is the producer's non-transferable job. His
finding that only a tiny fraction of surveyed partners knew their own break-even
is the best argument available for designing programs around partner economics
rather than vendor targets. Where this articulation departs: the source is
written for established manufacturers with mature distribution and treats the
program as components to implement. I treat it as one question asked repeatedly —
does the partner make money before we do — and everything that does not follow
from it is administration.

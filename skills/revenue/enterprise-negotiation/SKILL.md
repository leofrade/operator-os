# Enterprise Negotiation

> You are never negotiating with a company. You are arming one person to win an argument you will not be in the room for.

## The question this answers

- Who actually decides here, and what are they personally protecting?
- What happens to each side if this never closes — and who feels it more?
- The buyer said "we need to analyze it further." What did they mean?
- Do I concede on price, restructure the deal, or name the problem out loud?

## When to run it

Run it when the buying organization is large enough to have internal politics:
multiple stakeholders, an approval chain, procurement, someone whose career is
affected by the choice. Run it the moment a deal goes quiet, and before any
conversation where price will come up.

**Do not run this when** the deal is unqualified. Political mapping on an
opportunity with no confirmed problem, budget, or decision process is elaborate
self-deception — qualify first. And skip the influence layer entirely when the
buyer's problem is not real: applied to a bad fit, these techniques manufacture a
customer who churns and tells people why.

## The method

### 1. State your intent before you use any technique

The buyer assigns you an intention whether you declare one or not, and everything
you say afterward is filtered through it. Say the uncomfortable thing first:

> "My goal is to find out whether what you are dealing with is something we
> solve better than your alternatives. If not, I would rather know now than
> after we have both spent two months on it."

This is not softening. It buys the right to ask harder questions later and makes
walking away credible rather than a bluff.

### 2. Refuse to present the solution first

When the buyer says "show me how it works," the untrained response is a demo, and
the demo becomes the thing compared on price. Acknowledge, soften, redirect:
"happy to — so I show you the part that matters, what is happening right now that
made this a priority this quarter rather than last year?"

Success signal: within two minutes the buyer is describing their problem, not
evaluating your product. If not, you are in a procurement exercise and should
price accordingly.

### 3. Do the arithmetic with them, out loud, never for them

Five questions, in order: How do you measure this? What is the number today?
What would you want it to be? What is the difference worth? What is that over
two to three years?

Say the math out loud and invite correction. A buyer who computes the number with
you defends it internally; a buyer handed a number in a slide interrogates it.
Same figure, opposite outcome.

### 4. Map power per stakeholder, not per account

For each person who matters, fill this in. Labeled guesses are fine; blanks are
the finding.

```
NAME / ROLE
Above  — who evaluates them, and on what metric
Beside — who can veto (IT, legal, finance, ops)
Below  — who implements and can create friction
Posture — expanding, defending, or surviving
Their alternative if this does not happen
Their real interest, as distinct from their stated position
```

Two behavioral types need opposite materials. The **umbrella** absorbs risk
before passing it upward and needs defensible narrative: objective criteria,
comparable references, an argument that survives challenge. The **funnel** passes
pressure upward and needs a fast, visible win this quarter. Umbrella material
reads as slow to a funnel; funnel material reads as reckless to an umbrella.

Then the incentive question. Contracts get approved when they serve the metric
the approver is measured on — a newly promoted operations lead needs proof of
impact in ninety days, not a three-year cost of ownership model.

### 5. Compare alternatives honestly, both directions

Your leverage is the attractiveness of your best alternative if this dies. Theirs
is the same. Write both down. Sellers overestimate the buyer's options and
underestimate the cost of their status quo — the default outcome in enterprise
buying is not "choose a competitor," it is "do nothing for two more quarters,"
and nobody has priced that. If your own alternative is weak, recognize it before
you sit down. That awareness is what stops the unforced discount.

### 6. Convert positions into interests, then expand the deal

A position is what they say they want. An interest is why. Positions collide;
interests rarely do.

| Position | Likely interest | Structure that solves it |
|---|---|---|
| "We need 30% off" | Their approval limit sits below your price | Phase the contract into amounts they can approve alone |
| "We want a three-month trial" | Fear of being blamed for a failed rollout | Fixed-scope pilot, documented success criteria, performance guarantee |
| "Send it to procurement" | They have no political capital to spend | Find the person whose metric this moves and restart there |

Before dividing value, expand it: scope, phasing, payment structure, risk
allocation, support. Most price impasses are structure problems in costume.

### 7. Anchor on external criteria, and give them shelf arguments

Move from your will versus theirs to a standard neither of you controls:
regulatory exposure, an industry benchmark, the cost of one failure event, the
precedent of a comparable contract they already approved.

```
Weak:  "Our price is X and it does not go lower."
Right: "At your volume, a single incident of this type costs more than the
        annual contract. How are you pricing that risk today?"
```

Then hand them three to five facts they can quote internally. People decide
emotionally and justify rationally; supply the justification or they invent a
weaker one and lose the argument.

### 8. Name the warning sign before it costs you a quarter

Lost deals almost always had visible warning signs everyone politely ignored.
"Let me present this to the team first" means you do not have access to the
decision. "We will include it in next year's budget" means no budget and no
urgency. Silence after a proposal is the signal, not a period of internal
progress.

Say it in three parts: "I have a concern," then the observation stated flatly,
then "what do you think we should do about it?" A stopper found early is
information. Found late, it is a quarter.

## Failure modes

**Discounting before value is anchored** — price is conceded to keep a
conversation alive. Tell: the discount is offered to someone who does not control
the budget. → Nothing moves on price until the budget owner has agreed on the
cost of the problem.

**Symmetric alternative delusion** — assuming both sides feel equal pressure.
Tell: the seller thinks the buyer is choosing between vendors; the buyer is
choosing between acting and waiting. → Price the status quo. Doing nothing is
your main competitor and it is undefeated by default.

**Influence tactics as manipulation** — scarcity invented, social proof
exaggerated, reciprocity used as a debt claim. Tell: the "limited window" has
been extended twice. → Use only real scarcity: onboarding capacity, a regulatory
date, an implementation lead time. Manufactured urgency is detected, and
remembered.

**Single-threaded negotiation** — the deal runs through one contact with no
political capital. Tell: you cannot name who evaluates them, or on what metric.
→ Build a second relationship at another level before you need one.

**Reflexive defense under pressure** — the buyer attacks the proposal and the
seller defends it. Tell: the meeting becomes two people restating positions.
→ Do not push back on force. Ask what principle is behind the position, invite
the criticism, and let silence do work you would otherwise do badly.

**Politeness as a strategy** — everyone is warm, nothing is named, the deal ages.
Tell: three pleasant meetings, no commitment. → Redirect pressure instead of
removing it: "I built the timeline around the window you gave me. To hold it we
need confirmation by the 14th."

## Outputs

- A power map per stakeholder: accountability chain, veto points, posture,
  alternative, real interest
- The cost of the problem computed with the buyer, plus the cost of waiting a
  quarter
- Three to five shelf arguments the champion can use internally
- Two or three objective criteria that anchor price outside of opinion
- Active warning signs, with the exact sentence to name each one
- Deal-structure options — phasing, pilot scope, terms, risk allocation — drafted
  before any discount is considered

## Lineage

Three traditions, used for different jobs. Fisher, Ury, and Patton's *Getting to
Yes* supplies the structural core — alternatives as the source of real power,
interests underneath positions, expanding options before dividing them, anchoring
on objective standards. Robert Cialdini's *Influence* catalogs the psychological
triggers that operate whether or not you are aware of them. Mahan Khalsa's *Let's
Get Real or Let's Not Play* contributes the discipline that matters most in
practice: state your intent, refuse to present a solution before you understand
the problem, do the math with the client rather than for them, and name warning
signs out loud. Where this articulation departs: each
source treats negotiation as a two-party event. Enterprise deals are not. The
counterparty is a set of people with competing incentives, and the negotiation
you are actually running is the one your champion conducts after you leave — so
the deliverable is never a clever concession, it is ammunition someone else can
carry.

# Overcoming Indecision

> Once the buyer agrees things must change, the question that stops them is no longer "why change?" but "what if I get it wrong?" Answer that one.

## The question this answers

- Why do deals the buyer said yes to still end in "no decision"?
- What do I do when a buyer says "let me think about it"?
- Should I keep chasing this deal or walk away?
- Why does adding urgency make a stalled deal worse?

## When to run it

Run it after the buyer has agreed on the problem and the vision, and then hesitates: new stakeholders appear, a reference call is requested for the third time, the close date moves, or the buyer asks to "start over" on options. Watch for early signs from the first call, not only at the end.

**Do not run this when** the buyer still does not accept that the current state is a problem. That is a different fight; use an insight-led method (see `challenger-selling`) first. Also skip it for buyers who have already decided and are executing a defined procurement.

## The method

Study behind the numbers: about 2.5 million recorded sales calls analysed by a conversation-analytics vendor. The figures below are correlations from that single dataset, not guarantees.

### 0. Stop doing the default

When a buyer wavers, most reps re-sell the pain of the current state or add fear, urgency, scarcity or "everyone else is moving ahead". In the study this lowered outcomes in 84% of calls. The buyer already believes the problem is real; what they fear is being personally blamed for the purchase. More fear adds to the cause.

### 1. Judge — score the ability to decide, not only the ability to buy

**Find the source of the fear.** There can be more than one.

| Source | What you hear |
|---|---|
| Valuation problem (fear of picking wrong) | "We want it all", "what is the difference again?", "we are torn between these" |
| Missing information (fear of insufficient homework) | extra demos, references and expert calls beyond the norm; "overwhelmed", "still learning" |
| Outcome uncertainty (fear of not getting the result) | repeated ROI revisions, past purchases that burned them, requests for guarantees, doubt the result is reachable |

**Score the buyer on four reads**, 1 (decisive) to 5 (very indecisive) — this scoring is this repository's adaptation, not the book's official sheet:

1. How they consume information: normal diligence or research for research's sake? Do they backtrack late when new data appears?
2. How they compare options: can they say which criteria were must-have and how they built the shortlist, or are they comparing unlike things?
3. Whether "good enough" is acceptable: after a pilot, do they lead with what worked or with what failed?
4. What kind of delay: procrastination (still intends to buy; reschedules) versus decision avoidance ("not the right time", "priorities changed", "let's talk next quarter").

Add the amplifiers: high price, high personal consequence of error, and imposed deadlines.

**Decide.** High total score plus avoidance-type delay means deprioritize or disqualify, and take the deal out of this quarter's forecast. Test intent with one costly request, such as a meeting with legal, finance or procurement: agreement and a date means intent; evasion means avoidance.

### 2. Offer — recommend, do not interview

A confused buyer wants a guide, not more choice. Switch from "what matters to you?" to "here is what I would do in your position."

- Proactive guidance ("most customers like you start with this") lifted win rate from 18% to 44%.
- A personal recommendation ("if it were my decision, I would pick X") added about 74%.
- Open-ended discovery with no recommendation dropped conversion from 26% to 14% when the buyer was already confused.
- Present three tiers (large, medium, entry) and steer actively to the medium one.
- Earns the right only if you are already credible and unbiased; otherwise it reads as a closing move.

### 3. Limit — cap the research, do not feed it

No amount of information produces certainty. A working rule of thumb: decide with 40% to 70% of the information. Three moves:

- **Own the information flow.** Be the expert yourself; bring specialists in briefly, with a prep call, and take the conversation back. Recommend neutral reading that takes the buyer to the next level of understanding.
- **Anticipate objections.** An unanswered objection cut conversion from 31% to 17%. Pre-empt with "you may be wondering about X". Listen for weak acceptance ("I think so" instead of "got it") and stop to ask.
- **Be radically candid.** When the buyer asks for one more demo or reference, ask what they want to confirm and propose a faster path: "If this reference confirms the result, does that close it, or is something else holding you back?" Care about the buyer and challenge them directly; avoid agreeing and then complaining afterwards, pressing for your own interest, or staying silent to keep the peace.

Conversation tone that correlated with wins: reps talked more in wins than losses (58% vs 52%), acknowledged constantly, and used silence sparingly (8% to 17% of the call). Silence followed by visible confusion was toxic.

### 4. Take risk off the table

- **Set believable expectations**, not the theoretical maximum. Reps did this on 19% of calls; win rate was 51% with it and 20% without.
- **Offer downside protection.** Cancellation window, plan change, money-back guarantee, a carved-out scope with an exit clause, implementation support, or a project plan with owners, milestones and indicators agreed before signature. Win rate rose from 22% to 46%; reps used it on only 14% of calls.
- **Start smaller.** Recommend a narrower first scope with early wins and expansion afterwards. Remove options before quoting a price to avoid sticker shock.

### 5. Act as the buyer's agent

Buyers hold back because you know more than they do and are paid to sell. Close the gap by telling them not to overbuy, acknowledging what the product cannot yet do, crediting a competitor's real strength, and saying "I do not know, I will find out". Only then does asking for the business work: it makes "yes" the default instead of a push. In the study reps asked for the sale on 46% of calls — often because the right to ask had not been earned.

## Failure modes

| Failure | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Fear stacking | Discount deadlines and "others are moving ahead" after the buyer agreed | Stop; move to step 4 |
| Endless discovery | Re-asking needs for a buyer who is already confused | Recommend |
| Hope forecasting | A deal scored as avoidant stays in commit | Re-forecast, deprioritize |
| Fake guide | Recommendation always points to the largest package | Recommend the smaller scope; credibility is the asset |
| Silent specialist | Handing the call to an engineer for the whole meeting | Prep, brief entry, take it back |
| Forced close | Buyer signs but keeps checking alternatives afterwards | Fix expectations and exits; forced decisions often reverse |

## For managers

- Report "lost to inaction" separately from "lost to a no". The first is a seller-skill problem, not a product or messaging problem. In the study it ranged from 40% to 60% of deals.
- Set an age-out date per deal type; falling interaction frequency is a risk signal.
- Coach the middle of the team, where gains are largest, not only the tails.
- Score calls on a novice-to-master scale, not a tick-box.

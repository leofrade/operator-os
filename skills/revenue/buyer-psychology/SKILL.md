# Buyer Psychology

> You are not adapting to someone's personality. You are adapting to what they need in order to feel safe deciding.

## The question this answers

- Why did a strong meeting produce silence and no objection?
- Should I send the analysis before the call or open with the outcome?
- This buyer keeps agreeing and never advances. What is actually happening?
- Four stakeholders in one room want four different things. Who do I write for?

## When to run it

Run it before a first meeting with someone you have not sold to, when a deal
stalls without a stated reason, and when a proposal will be read by one person
and forwarded to another with different instincts.

**Do not run this when** you are using it as a substitute for a business case.
No amount of style-matching sells to someone who does not have the problem. Drop
it when you have direct evidence: if a buyer has told you how they want to be
sold to, believe them over any framework. And never say a buyer's type out loud —
once it is a label on a person rather than a read on a situation, it does damage.

## The method

**Discount the model before you use it.** Four-quadrant behavioral typing has weak
predictive validity and is not accepted personality science. The quadrants are not
stable traits, the instruments that assign them replicate poorly, and the same
person lands in different boxes across vendors, across contexts, and across a bad
week. Nobody has shown that knowing a buyer's quadrant predicts what they buy. So
the operational value is narrow: this is a conversational adaptation heuristic you
run in the moment, on observed behavior, and drop the second the behavior
contradicts it. It is not a classification of a person, and treating it as one
makes you a worse seller, because you stop watching. What survives the discount is
the back half of this method — the stress behaviors in step 4, which turn an
ambiguous silence into something you can act on, and the objection translation in
step 5, because the same sentence really does mean different things from different
people. The front half is scaffolding for those two, not a taxonomy worth
defending.

### 1. Place them on two axes using observable evidence only

Two questions, answered from what you have seen: emails, calls, public posts, how
they run a meeting.

- **Orientation** — people and relationships, or tasks and process?
- **Posture** — dominant and directive, or receptive and accommodating?

That produces four working archetypes. Treat them as tendencies with confidence
levels, not as identities.

| | Dominant | Receptive |
|---|---|---|
| **People-oriented** | **Promoter** — talks first, decides fast, wants to be impressed and visible | **Supporter** — warm, consensus-seeking, decides slowly, avoids conflict |
| **Task-oriented** | **Driver** — direct, impatient, wants the result and control of the decision | **Analyst** — methodical, evidence-driven, decides after documented comparison |

### 2. Read the first three minutes and update

Your pre-call hypothesis is worth less than the first exchange. Four tells:

| Signal | Promoter | Driver | Supporter | Analyst |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opens with | A story | The agenda | A question about you | A prepared question list |
| Values first | Energy, novelty | Competence, results | Trust, comfort | Precision, preparation |
| Reacts to a proposal | "Tell me more" | "What is the result?" | "Let me talk to the team" | "Send me the documentation" |
| Loses interest when | It is predictable | It is slow | It is pressured | It is vague |

Adjust mid-meeting. Being wrong for three minutes costs nothing; being wrong for
three months costs the deal.

### 3. Change the artifact, not your personality

This is where the framework earns its keep, and where most people misapply it.
You are not performing a character. You are changing the format of the evidence
and the speed at which it arrives.

| | Send before | Open with | Close on |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Promoter** | A short, striking data point | What is possible and who else is doing it | Momentum and visibility |
| **Driver** | A one-page executive summary with numbers | The result, in one sentence, in the first minute | Three options they choose between |
| **Supporter** | Nothing heavy; a friendly agenda | Their team, their day, their constraints | A small, low-risk first step |
| **Analyst** | The full technical material, days early | A specific finding about their situation | A documented implementation plan |

The Driver wants options because a single recommendation feels like losing
control. The Analyst wants to see where a competitor is stronger, because one
honest weakness buys credibility for everything else. The Supporter needs to not
be asked to decide alone. The Promoter needs the decision to be interesting.

### 4. Predict how each one fails under pressure

Every archetype has a stress behavior, and every stress behavior signals that you
are pushing on the wrong axis. This is the most useful part of the model: it
converts an ambiguous silence into a diagnosis.

| Under pressure they | Escalates to | Do this |
|---|---|---|
| **Promoter** talks more, digresses | Outburst, drama | Slow down, reconnect, stop presenting |
| **Driver** grabs control, interrupts | Flat declarations: "this does not work" | Concede control, re-present as choices |
| **Supporter** agrees with everything | Silent withdrawal, then ghosting | Remove pressure, ask what is worrying them |
| **Analyst** goes quiet, stops asking | Complete shutdown | Stop talking, send written material, wait |

The dangerous one is the Supporter. Agreement is their conflict-avoidance
behavior, so the most agreeable meeting in your pipeline may be the one closest
to death.

### 5. Translate the objection before answering it

The same sentence means different things from different people. Answer the
meaning, not the words.

| "I need to think about it" from a | Actually means | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Promoter | The excitement has faded | Give them something worth showing off |
| Driver | Not worth their time yet | One sentence on the outcome, then stop talking |
| Supporter | Uneasy and will not say so | "Something is bothering you — what is it?" |
| Analyst | Exactly what they said | Send the comparison, book the follow-up, do not push |

"It is expensive" works the same way: from a Driver it is a challenge about
return, from an Analyst a request for the cost breakdown, from a Promoter a sign
they do not want it enough yet, and from a Supporter usually a proxy for a risk
they cannot articulate.

### 6. In a mixed room, write for the decision-maker and de-risk for the veto

You cannot run four motions at once. Pick the person who owns the decision and
set pace and framing for them. Then make sure nothing gives anyone else a reason
to block: the Analyst gets the appendix, the Supporter gets reassurance that
their team was considered, the Driver gets the summary page. The goal in a group
is not to persuade everyone — it is to persuade one person and remove every
objection the others could raise.

## Failure modes

**Typecasting** — the read hardens into a label and the seller stops observing.
Tell: you describe a buyer by type in internal notes and never revise it. → Carry
a confidence level, not a verdict. Most people are a dominant tendency plus a
secondary one that appears under stress.

**Style as a substitute for substance** — perfectly adapted delivery of a weak
case. Tell: excellent rapport, no business case, no movement. → Adaptation
changes format and pace. It does not create value. Build the argument first.

**Mistaking role for style** — assuming every finance leader is an Analyst and
every founder is a Promoter. Tell: your read came from a job title. → Titles
correlate weakly. Use observed behavior; absent that, expect to be wrong a third
of the time.

**Reading calm behavior only** — the read comes from relaxed interactions and
collapses when the deal gets tense. Tell: your buyer behaves like a different
person once the proposal lands. → Pressure behavior is the more useful signal.
Plan for it before it arrives.

**Mirroring that becomes mimicry** — the seller performs a personality instead of
adjusting evidence. Tell: it feels theatrical to you, so it reads as false to
them. → Change what you send and how fast you move. Do not change who you are;
buyers detect the seam.

**Optimizing for your own comfort** — the seller runs their preferred motion and
calls it authenticity. Tell: every deck is the same regardless of audience.
→ What works for you to sell is not what works for everyone to buy. That is the
entire point of the model.

**One message for four profiles** — a proposal engineered to satisfy everyone,
satisfying none. Tell: four competing tones in one document. → One primary
audience, plus appendices that neutralize the others.

## Outputs

- A profile hypothesis per stakeholder, with confidence level and the evidence
- A pre-meeting plan: what to send in advance, how to open, what pace to hold
- A proposal reformatted for the decision-maker, with appendix material for the
  likely veto
- A translated objection table — what their stated objections probably mean
- Stress signals to watch for, with the de-escalation move for each
- A group strategy: primary audience, and what each other party needs to not block

## Lineage

Four-quadrant behavioral models of this kind descend from William Moulton
Marston's work on behavioral dimensions, later commercialized as DISC, and from
David Merrill and Roger Reid's social styles research, which established the
two-axis structure — assertiveness against responsiveness — that nearly every
sales adaptation still uses. The labels vary by vendor and are largely
interchangeable.

Both lineages should be discounted. Marston's dimensions were never validated as a
measurement instrument, and the commercial descendants — DISC, social styles, and
the sales frameworks built on them — persist on face validity and licensing
revenue, not on evidence that they predict behavior. Reviews of this class of
typology find low test-retest stability and little predictive power. I keep the
structure anyway, for the reason a seller keeps any rough heuristic: adapting
format and pace to observed behavior costs nothing and occasionally saves a deal.
That is a much smaller claim than the vendors make, and the only one this file
supports.

Where this articulation departs: most sales versions spend their
length describing the four types and supplying scripts, which encourages
typecasting and produces sellers performing personalities. The useful content is
elsewhere — in the stress behaviors, because they turn silence into a diagnosis,
and in the translation of objections, because the same sentence means four
different things depending on who says it.

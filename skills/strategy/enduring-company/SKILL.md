# Enduring Company

> Discipline about who you hire and what you refuse to do compounds. The study that made this famous does not survive its own method — so take the sequence and leave the certainty.

## The question this answers

- We are good and have stopped getting better. What is the mechanism of the plateau?
- Who should we promote into senior leadership, and what do we measure to decide?
- Why does bad news reach me late, and what structure would change that?
- What should we simply stop doing, and where does the freed budget go?

## When to run it

Run this when the company is past survival, has real customers and cash flow, and has settled into competence — the plateau where every year resembles the last with slightly better numbers. Run it before a leadership transition, and when leadership keeps being surprised by what frontline staff already knew.

**Do not run this when** you are pre-fit or fighting for cash. These disciplines assume you know what business you are in; applied to a company still searching, they cement the wrong answer. Also do not run it as a change program with a launch and a name — companies that announce a transformation usually get the announcement and nothing else.

## The method

### Move 1 — Discount the evidence, keep the mechanism

Know what this material is before using it. The research selected companies that had already outperformed, then looked backward for shared traits. That design cannot separate causes from correlates, and cannot tell you how many companies did the same things and failed — those were never in the sample. History was unkind to the exemplars: one of the eleven liquidated in bankruptcy within a decade, another entered government conservatorship in 2008, another spent years in a conduct scandal that cost its leadership, several reverted to average. The broader critique — that studies of admired companies measure the halo following good results rather than its causes — is well established and, in my view, correct.

That does not make the ideas worthless; it changes their status. Treat each finding as a hypothesis about your company, not a law. The mechanisms below survive because each has an independent argument for why it works. Run each as an experiment with an observable, and drop what moves nothing visible in two quarters.

### Move 2 — Sequence people before strategy, and mean it

The order is the claim: right people in the right seats, then decide where to go. Not because people matter more than strategy — everyone says that — but because a team assembled around a strategy cannot change it, since changing it invalidates half the hires. A team selected for judgment, adaptability, and standards absorbs three pivots.

Three rules make it real. In doubt on a hire, do not hire — and accept that growth is capped by the rate at which you find people who clear the bar. When a change is needed, make it, after checking whether the person is in the wrong seat rather than the wrong company. And put your strongest people on your largest opportunities, not your largest problems; most companies do the reverse and staff their future with whoever is left.

### Move 3 — Make leadership selection a measurable filter

The profile that matters combines personal modesty with an unreasonable, sustained standard for the institution. It is not a personality type — quiet is no proxy for humble, and charisma is not disqualifying. Convert it into observable behavior and score senior candidates.

```
WINDOW AND MIRROR TEST — score 0-3 per line, from evidence not self-report
  After a win, credits team, timing, luck
  After a loss, looks at themselves before circumstances
  Has developed a successor who outperformed them
  Has held an unpopular standard under pressure for more than a quarter
  Expresses ambition for the institution, not the seat
Rule: below 10/15 on a senior hire, keep looking. Below 8, it is a no.
```

This is the weakest part of the original research and the most contaminated by hindsight — leaders of successful companies get described as humble and determined regardless. Use the filter anyway: people who take responsibility for failure produce more accurate information, and people who develop successors reduce key-person risk.

### Move 4 — Build the machinery that forces bad news upward

Confronting reality is not a virtue, it is a plumbing problem. Information stops flowing up for structural reasons — the sender is punished, the receiver signals a preferred answer, nobody owns the channel. Four fixes, in order of leverage: lead with questions rather than conclusions, so people are not guessing at your position; run blameless postmortems whose output is a mechanism change, not an owner; give someone standing authority to escalate to you regardless of hierarchy; build red-flag mechanisms that make information impossible to ignore, like a metric that trips an automatic review.

Hold two things at once: unconditional confidence that the company prevails, and unflinching accuracy about conditions now. The failure mode is not pessimism — it is the optimism that keeps setting dates for the recovery and breaking on each one.

### Move 5 — Define the focus test, then use it as a budget instrument

Write three answers and treat their intersection as your operating boundary. What can you become genuinely best at, in a market defined narrowly enough for the claim to be true — not what you are good at now, not what you wish. What single economic denominator drives results — profit per customer, per employee, per location, per transaction — where a 10% improvement moves the business most. And what will this team sustain for ten years without external motivation.

This arrives through iteration over years, not in a workshop. Run a standing group of five to twelve people who know different parts of the business deeply, debating for understanding rather than position, seeking no consensus, leaving the decision with you. For clarity faster, increase cycles per year, not meeting length.

Then convert it into a stop-doing list — the part that pays in the first quarter. A budget's real function is not allocating a percentage to each activity; it is deciding which are fully funded and which end. A once-in-a-lifetime opportunity outside the boundary is still outside the boundary.

### Move 6 — Push a measured flywheel

Identify the four to six steps where each makes the next easier, and confirm the loop closes — the last step's output feeds the first. Instrument each step and push in one direction. There is no single decisive push. The observable: each turn requires less input for the same output. If the loop is not getting cheaper, it is a process diagram, not a flywheel. Alignment follows momentum and cannot be manufactured ahead of it.

## Failure modes

**Cargo-culting the exemplars** — practices are copied from admired firms without asking whether the practice caused the outcome. → Apply Move 1's discount to every borrowed practice, including these.

**Best-in-the-world as vanity** — the market gets defined broadly enough to sound impressive and the claim becomes untestable. → Narrow it until someone is uncomfortable. "Best at X for Y buyers in Z" beats "best-in-class platform."

**Humility read as passivity** — the profile selects agreeable leaders who avoid conflict, and the ferocious institutional standard disappears. → Score both halves. A candidate who has never held an unpopular position under pressure fails, whatever the references say.

**Brutal-facts theater** — postmortems happen, blame is avoided, no mechanism changes, and the same failure recurs six months later under a new name. → Require each postmortem to output one mechanism change with an owner and a date. Track recurrence.

**Discipline that lives in rules or in one person** — controls multiply because the disciplined people were never hired, or a leader imposes rigor personally and it reverts when they leave. → More controls means a people problem wearing a process costume. Test the rest by absence: if standards drop while that leader is on vacation, you have a person, not a system.

## Outputs

- Which claims here you treat as tested and which as hypotheses, with the observable for each
- A scored window-and-mirror assessment for every senior hire or promotion
- A named escalation channel, a red-flag list, and a postmortem format that outputs mechanism changes
- A written focus boundary: the best-at claim, the economic denominator, the ten-year commitment
- A stop-doing list with the budget released, and a flywheel map with a cost-per-turn trend

## Lineage

Jim Collins and his research team produced *Good to Great* from a multi-year study of large US public companies, isolating eleven that sustained outperformance against matched peers. The sequence of disciplined people, then thought, then action; the leadership profile combining modesty with institutional will; the three-circle focus test; and the flywheel are his. *Built to Last*, with Jerry Porras, supplies the companion ideas on preserving a core while stimulating change.

This articulation departs on epistemics. The design selects on the outcome and cannot rule out that the observed traits are consequences of success rather than causes, and the exemplars' later record is bad enough that presenting the findings as settled would mislead. So the first move is a discount and every remaining move is a hypothesis with an observable attached. What survives is the most useful part anyway: hire before you strategize, build plumbing for bad news, write down what you will not do.

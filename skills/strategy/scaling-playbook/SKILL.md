# Scaling Playbook

> After product/market fit, the company that wins is rarely the one with the better product. It is the one that builds distribution and a functioning organization before the market notices what worked.

## The question this answers

- We have product/market fit and we are still not winning. What is actually behind?
- The CEO is the bottleneck on six decisions a week. What comes off the calendar first?
- How do I hire executives without over-hiring for a stage we are not at, or under-hiring someone who caps the org in a year?
- How do we run a reorg without losing a month to lobbying?

## When to run it

Run this when product/market fit is demonstrated — retention holds, customers pull rather than get pushed, demand exceeds your capacity to serve it — and the company is between twenty and a few hundred people. Run it again at each doubling; the answers change.

**Do not run this when** product/market fit is assumed rather than evidenced. Everything here makes an organization bigger and more expensive. Applied before fit, it burns the runway you needed to find fit while making the company slower to change direction. If you are still learning who the customer is, the move is fewer people, not better-organized ones.

## The method

### Move 1 — Diagnose which of the three post-fit jobs is behind

There are exactly three, and they compete for the same leadership attention.

| Job | The test | The symptom when it is behind |
|---|---|---|
| Take the market | Are you selling past early adopters into the segment's mainstream? | Growth decelerating while the category grows; unknown competitors appearing in deals |
| Get to the next product | Is a second revenue line in real customers' hands? | Single-product concentration; a roadmap of increments |
| Build everything else | Do finance, people, and legal have owners who are good at them? | Self-inflicted incidents — payroll, compliance, an impossible offer letter |

Score each and fund the worst. The characteristic mistake of technically strong founders is spending every quarter on the second job while the first quietly decides the outcome. Early adopters are a small fraction of any market and behave nothing like the majority. They find you. Nobody else will.

### Move 2 — Audit the CEO's calendar as a capacity problem

The founder's time is the scarcest asset in the company and the least examined. Print last month's calendar and mark each block: only-I-can-do-this, someone-could-with-coaching, or should-not-be-happening. First-round interviews, most internal functional meetings, most partnership conversations, and nearly all conference invitations are the third.

The tell for a CEO who is not delegating: leaving meetings with action items for themselves, still doing the work of functions that have owners, needing to be on every thread. The correction is not willpower; it is hiring the person the work belongs to and tolerating a worse version than you would have produced. Re-audit monthly until the ratio stabilizes, then quarterly.

### Move 3 — Hire executives for the next 12 to 18 months

Shorter than twelve months and you re-hire the role immediately. Longer than eighteen and you have bought someone who is bored, imports process the company cannot metabolize, and leaves within a year.

Before opening the search, have coffee with three to five genuinely excellent people in that function to calibrate what excellent looks like. Without that benchmark you will hire the best candidate you saw rather than a good one. Write the criteria down and give them to every interviewer.

Evaluate six traits: functional depth practitioners respect; ability to recruit and manage a team in that function; collegiality under conflicting incentives; communication across functions and to the board; ownership complete enough that you stop thinking about the area; and first-principles reasoning rather than replay of the last company. Assume one or two of these hires will be wrong. Correcting in three months costs a quarter; tolerating it for a year costs the team.

### Move 4 — Reorganize decisively or not at all

Structure is a pragmatic choice, not a correct answer, and it changes again in six to twelve months. Optimize for two things: who has the bandwidth to actually run the area, and who breaks ties — people who disagree regularly should share a manager close enough to decide.

Execute in sequence: decide why, in one sentence; design for the next six to twelve months; get private buy-in from the few leaders directly affected; brief every manager so they can answer questions; know where essentially every person lands before you speak; announce and implement inside twenty-four hours. A reorg that leaks and lingers produces a week of lobbying, a month of rumor, and no work.

### Move 5 — Shift from product mindset to distribution mindset

The product got you the customer base. From here the customer base is the asset. Three consequences.

Price higher. Price is the most under-used growth lever, and the objection to raising it usually comes from the people who built the product rather than the people who buy it. A higher price funds better distribution, which buys share, which funds more product. It is also the cleanest moat test: if customers will not pay a premium, you do not have one.

Push new products through the channel you already own rather than acquiring a new audience for each. And accept that you cannot build everything — companies at real scale systematically under-acquire. Acquisitions come in three shapes: a team, a product that fills a roadmap hole, or a strategic asset you cannot reproduce. The first two are near-formulaic. The third is a relationship built over quarters before any offer.

### Move 6 — Install the boring functions before the incident

Between roughly fifty and a hundred and fifty people, informal coordination stops working — the group exceeds the relationships people can hold. That is the window for a real people function, not a recruiter with an expanded title. Legal exists to tell the CEO where the line is before it is crossed; finance, so the company does not run out of money for reasons unrelated to the business. These are the easiest things to defer and the most expensive, because the cost arrives as one avoidable catastrophe rather than as gradual friction.

## Failure modes

**Early-adopter complacency** — the first cohort found you, loved the product, and made demand look self-generating. The mainstream of the segment behaves and buys differently. → Build deliberate distribution capacity while the early cohort still makes the numbers look fine. By the time deceleration shows in the metrics, a competitor has spent two quarters on it.

**Over-hiring the executive** — a senior leader from a company ten times your size joins to run a team of eight, brings the process that worked there, and cannot get traction. → Hire for 12–18 months of scale, with an explicit conversation up front about what happens when the role outgrows the person.

**The slow reorg** — leadership consults broadly, the plan leaks, and the company spends three weeks negotiating with itself. → Private buy-in from the few directly affected, then announce and implement inside a day. Broad consultation on structure is not participation, it is a land grab.

**The loyalty tax** — an early employee who was excellent at fifteen people is struggling at two hundred, and nobody says anything because of history. The team notices long before leadership acts and reads the silence as the standard. → Have the conversation. Some early people scale and should be given more; some need a different seat; some need a respectful exit.

**Underpricing as strategy** — the price was set when the product was worse and nobody has revisited it. → Test a higher price on new customers. Willingness to pay is the moat diagnostic.

**Building everything in-house** — every acquisition proposal meets "we could build that," and the roadmap hole stays open for two years. → Maintain a standing acquisition roadmap sourced from hiring managers, product leaders, and the executive team. The default error at scale is buying too little.

## Outputs

- A scored diagnosis of the three post-fit jobs, with the worst one funded
- A marked-up CEO calendar with a stop-doing list and named owners for what moves
- Written executive hiring criteria, calibrated externally, distributed to all interviewers
- A reorg plan with the one-sentence why, every person's landing spot, and a 24-hour execution window
- A pricing review with a tested increase, and a distribution plan for the next product
- A standing acquisition roadmap by type: team, product, strategic
- A staffing plan for people, finance, and legal tied to headcount thresholds

## Lineage

Elad Gil assembled the *High Growth Handbook* from operating experience at Google and Twitter during periods of extreme headcount growth, plus advisory work across many companies, with interviews including Marc Andreessen, Claire Hughes Johnson, Sam Altman, and Patrick Collison. The three post-fit imperatives, the executive-hiring calibration technique, and the argument that distribution outlasts product as a moat originate there.

This articulation departs by turning a reference handbook into a sequence with a diagnostic first step. The book is deliberately non-linear — chapters you consult as problems arrive. That is useful for a reader and useless for an operator who does not yet know which problem they have. The scored three-job diagnosis, run before anything else, is the addition.

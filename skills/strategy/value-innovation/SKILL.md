# Value Innovation

> Differentiation and low cost are only a trade-off if you accept the industry's current list of things worth competing on.

## The question this answers

- Why do we keep winning deals on price and losing money on every one of them?
- We score higher than every competitor on every attribute we measure. Why is growth flat?
- What could we delete from the offer that no buyer would notice, and what would that fund?
- Where is the demand that is in nobody's pipeline?

## When to run it

Run it when the category has converged: three or more players with near-identical feature lists, win rates decided by discount, and a sales cycle where the buyer's first question is price. Run it when you are building the second product and want it out of the incumbent's comparison grid. Run it annually as a check if you already hold a differentiated position.

**Do not run this when** you have no repeatable sale yet. A pre-PMF company has no cost structure to restructure and no industry curve to diverge from; you are looking for a first buyer, not a new market boundary. Do not run it when the honest diagnosis is execution — you lose because your reps are weaker or your product breaks. Value innovation cannot rescue a company that is simply worse at the same game. And do not run it if leadership will not authorize deleting anything; without the delete, the exercise produces a more expensive product with a new adjective.

## The method

**Move 1 — Chart what the category actually sells on.**
List 5 to 12 competing factors. An attribute earns a place on the list only if a buyer used it unprompted in a won or lost deal review. Internal jargon on the axis is diagnostic: it means the strategy is written from the inside out. Score yourself and two or three comparators 1–5 on each and draw the lines.

Decision rule: if your line tracks a competitor within one point on more than 70% of attributes, you do not have a strategy, you have a market-share fight. Proceed. If your line already diverges sharply and you are profitable, stop — you are in the maintenance case, jump to Move 5 and set a re-check date.

**Move 2 — Spend the delete budget before the build budget.**
Four questions, in this order, and the first two are not optional:

| Action | What it forces |
|---|---|
| Eliminate | Which factors does the industry take as mandatory that have stopped carrying value? |
| Reduce | Where are you over-serving to beat rivals nobody is scoring you against? |
| Raise | Which factor is the industry rationing below what buyers would pay for? |
| Create | What does the category not offer at all? |

Threshold: the Eliminate and Reduce columns together must remove at least 20% of unit cost or delivery effort. If they do not, you have written an "and more" strategy, which is a price increase wearing a strategy costume. Go back and cut harder.

**Move 3 — Probe three boundaries, not six.**
Most reframing exercises generate slides. Three probes generate decisions:

1. *The alternative.* What does a buyer do when they buy nobody in your category — a spreadsheet, an internal hire, doing nothing? The factors that make them choose that path are the ones worth attacking.
2. *The buyer chain.* Separate who pays, who uses, and who blocks. Categories fixate on one of the three for decades; the trapped value is usually held by another. A vertical SaaS in a regulated market that sells to compliance officers and ignores the operator doing the work daily is the standard version of this mistake.
3. *The whole job.* Map before, during, and after. Where does the buyer's total cost sit relative to your price? If maintenance or rework is three times the purchase price, that is where the offer should be redesigned.

**Move 4 — Run four gates in a fixed order: utility, price, cost, adoption.**
Each is pass/fail. A fail sends you back to Move 2, never forward.

- *Utility.* The new curve must remove a blocking obstacle in the buying or usage cycle, not add a convenience. Convenience does not move a purchase decision that is currently "no".
- *Price.* Set the price from what the buyer's alternative costs them, before you know your cost. Anchor on the mass of target buyers, not the top decile.
- *Cost.* `target cost = strategic price − required margin`. If you cannot reach the target cost inside twelve months using levers you control — process redesign, partnership, a change in the pricing mechanism rather than the price level — the strategy is fiction.
- *Adoption.* Name three parties who lose: usually your own sales force's commission plan, a channel partner, and one internal function whose headcount the new model shrinks. Write the answer for each before launch.

**Move 5 — Compress to one sentence.**
State the strategy in a sentence a competitor could not credibly say. If they could say it, focus failed and you are back at Move 2. Set the re-check trigger now: when discounting rises two points year over year, or when a rival's curve comes within one point of yours, re-run from Move 1.

## Failure modes

**The "and more" curve** — every factor raised, none eliminated, cost per unit up. The tell is a deck that says "best of both worlds." → Approve no item in the Create column until the Eliminate column carries a real number.

**Axis written by the product team** — the competing factors are module counts and uptime tiers. The tell: no buyer used those words in the last ten calls. → Rebuild the list from deal-review transcripts only, in the buyer's vocabulary.

**"Blue ocean" as a synonym for niche** — same buyer, same budget line, same competitors, narrower slice. → Test it: are you funded from a different budget line, or displacing "do nothing"? If neither, you did segmentation and should call it that.

**Pricing from cost** — the price is computed after the cost is known, and lands just under the incumbent's. The tell: your pricing memo starts with a margin target. → Price gate before cost gate, always. Cost adapts or the idea dies.

**Skipping the adoption gate** — the strategy is sound and the field refuses to sell it because it halves their commission. → Compensation and channel economics are part of the design, not a rollout detail.

**Copying someone else's delete list** — implementing the eliminations from a famous case in a category with different economics. → The four questions are a generator, not an answer key.

**Winning once and never re-charting** — a decade of dominance, competitors converge, and the curve is now the industry average. The tell: stable win rate with slowly rising discounts. → Convergence is the trigger to re-run, and it shows up in discount data before it shows up in revenue.

## Outputs

- A scored attribute chart with your curve and two or three comparators
- An eliminate / reduce / raise / create table where the left half carries a quantified cost reduction
- Three boundary-probe findings, each with the specific buyer behavior that supports it
- A four-gate memo: pass/fail per gate, the strategic price, the target-cost math, and named adoption blockers with owners
- A one-sentence strategy statement plus the reason a named competitor cannot say it
- A re-check trigger written into the planning calendar

## Lineage

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne built this out of a long study of strategic moves across industries, arguing that the unit of analysis is the move rather than the firm, and that differentiation and low cost can be pursued together when a company rewrites what its category competes on. Their sequencing of utility, price, cost, and adoption is the part that has held up best under pressure. This articulation departs from theirs in two ways: I collapse their six boundary reframings into the three that have ever changed a decision I was in the room for, and I treat the cost target as a hard gate rather than a planning step, because that is where most attempts quietly die while everyone is still admiring the new curve.

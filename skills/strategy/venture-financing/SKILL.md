# Venture Financing

> The valuation is the only term negotiated in public. The terms that decide who gets paid, who decides, and what happens when things go badly are negotiated in the parts founders skim.

## The question this answers

- Should we take venture capital at all, or is the fund's return math incompatible with what we are building?
- How much do we raise, and against whose proof point — ours or the next investor's?
- What does this term sheet actually change about my life, term by term?
- What does the cap table look like at a bad exit, and who eats a down round?

## When to run it

Run this before your first institutional conversation, again when a term sheet lands, and whenever the trajectory diverges from the plan the round was priced on. Also run it when someone proposes a bridge — bridges are financings with all the consequences and none of the scrutiny.

**Do not run this when** the business can reach durable profitability on revenue or debt, when the credible ceiling on enterprise value is in the low hundreds of millions, or when you are unwilling to share governance for a decade. Venture funds are built around a small number of very large outcomes: roughly half of a fund's investments return little or nothing, and the economics depend on a handful returning multiples of the entire fund. That math is not hostile to you, but it is indifferent to a good mid-sized business.

**This is not legal advice.** Nothing here substitutes for counsel qualified in your jurisdiction, and the mechanics below describe US-style venture practice — a local convention, not a law of nature.

## The method

### Move 1 — Test company-fund fit before pitch fit

Ask what must be true for your best investor to return their fund on you. If they deploy $10M for 20% and need that position to return $300M, the company has to reach roughly $1.5B. Can you argue that credibly — not enthusiastically, credibly — inside ten years? If not, you will spend a decade being managed toward a shape the business cannot take. Smaller funds with lower thresholds are a legitimate answer; so is not raising.

### Move 2 — Size the round against the next round's evidence

Do not size against your burn. Size against what the next investor must see to price a step-up, then add buffer. Work backwards: the next round's thesis requires proof; that proof requires certain hires over certain months; that is your number, plus 25–40%.

The worst combination available is a small round at a high price — a high-water mark you must grow into without the resources to do it. Target roughly a 2x step between rounds. A clean up-and-to-the-right graph is worth more than any single high mark.

### Move 3 — Read the sheet in three stacks

Sort every term into economics, control, or downside. For each, write the sentence that says what changes for you.

| Term | What it actually changes for the founder |
|---|---|
| Post-money valuation | Sets the bar for the next round. Post-money usually includes the new pool and converting notes, so your dilution exceeds the headline |
| Option pool | A percentage of post-money, typically carved out pre-money, so existing holders fund it. Size to a real 12–18 month hiring plan |
| 1x non-participating preference | Standard. Money back or conversion, not both. Costs little at good outcomes |
| Participating preference | Money back *and* a share of the rest. At mediocre exits, the difference between a life-changing outcome and nothing. Anything above 1x is a loan with equity upside |
| Broad-based weighted average | Standard down-round protection. Moderate extra dilution to common |
| Full ratchet | Reprices the old round at the new low price. In a real down round this can halve founders and employees |
| Protective provisions | Veto over new rounds, sale, liquidation, pool increases. Keep the vote unified across all preferred, or a small later investor gains a veto over your exit |
| Board composition | Who can fire you. This term determines whether every other term gets renegotiated later |
| Pro rata rights | Existing investors' claim on the next round; can crowd out the new lead |
| No-shop | Usually 30 days of exclusivity. Run your process before signing |

### Move 4 — Model the bad outcomes before signing the good one

Build two artifacts. A payout matrix: exit values from below invested capital to a strong outcome, showing what each class receives and the breakeven above which common holders receive anything. And a down-round simulation: the same round priced 50% lower, under both anti-dilution formulas. Full ratchet can take a founding team from majority to small minority in one financing. If a term only hurts in scenarios you have decided cannot happen, you have not modeled it — you have assumed it away.

### Move 5 — Negotiate three things, concede the rest, then run the board

Pick the three terms that matter most — usually board composition, preference structure, and pool sizing — and trade everything else visibly and cheerfully. Founders who contest twenty terms lose the three that mattered and acquire a reputation for being difficult with someone who will sit on their board for eight years.

Then treat the board as an instrument, not an audience. Materials in advance, meeting time spent on the two or three decisions that need judgment, bad news early and with a plan. A board that learns about problems late stops advising and starts managing.

### Move 6 — Adjust for jurisdiction

US venture practice assumes a Delaware C corporation, multiple share classes, deep corporate case law, and liquid secondary and IPO markets. Much of that does not travel. In many jurisdictions the default private company form does not support preferred stock cleanly, so early financings run through convertible instruments and only become equity on conversion. Employee option plans may lack a tax-efficient equivalent, changing how you compensate. Drag-along, liquidation preference, and transfer restrictions may be enforceable only as contract between shareholders rather than as corporate rights — which matters enormously when someone refuses to sign. Fiduciary doctrines shaping US board behavior in a sale have no direct analogue in most civil-law systems.

The consequence: many international companies eventually place a holding company in a jurisdiction investors recognize. Do that early if you plan to raise from foreign funds — retroactive restructuring is slow, expensive, and sometimes taxable.

## Failure modes

**Optimizing the headline number** — the founder takes the highest price available. Two years later the company has grown well and still cannot clear its own last mark; the next round is flat or down, employees see it, morale breaks. → Price for a step-up you can beat. Underwriting your own next round is part of the job.

**The small round at a high price** — insufficient capital to hit the milestones the price implies. → Take more money at that price, or that money at a lower price. The combination has no good path out.

**Note stacking** — a $1M seed grows through rolling closes into $3M of convertible instruments at different caps and discounts. Conversion at the priced round produces dilution nobody modeled, discovered at signing. → Keep a live conversion model updated at every close, and cap total pre-priced instruments.

**Reading economics and skipping control** — valuation is negotiated hard, then per-series protective provisions and an unfavorable board are accepted. A minority investor from three rounds ago can now block your acquisition. → Give control terms the same attention as price.

**The theoretical down round** — full ratchet or participating preference is accepted for a better headline, because the bad scenario is treated as implausible. → Model it at 50% down. If you cannot live with the output, it is not a concession, it is a bet.

**Bridging instead of resetting** — a bridge buys six months, does not change the underlying problem, and stacks preference above everyone. → Recapitalize honestly instead: reduce preference, refresh the pool, reset incentives. Painful once beats degrading indefinitely.

## Outputs

- A go / no-go on the venture path with the fund-return math written out
- A round size derived from the next round's proof points
- Every term sorted into economics, control, or downside, each with its plain-language consequence
- A payout matrix across exit scenarios, including the common breakeven
- A down-round simulation under both anti-dilution formulas
- A ranked negotiation list: three terms to win, everything else tradeable
- For non-US companies, a structuring question list for local counsel

## Lineage

Scott Kupor wrote *Secrets of Sand Hill Road* from inside Andreessen Horowitz, to remove the information asymmetry that historically favored investors in founder negotiations. The structure of the venture ecosystem, the power-law return math, and the term-by-term walkthrough are his, and it remains the clearest explanation of why venture investors behave as they do.

This articulation departs in two places. It is a decision procedure with modeling artifacts rather than an explanation of an industry — the payout matrix and the down-round simulation are the deliverables, not the glossary. And it treats US practice as one jurisdiction's convention rather than as the shape of venture financing everywhere, because most operators reading this are not incorporated in Delaware and will pay for that assumption.

# Disruption Radar

> Most things called disruption are competition. The distinction is expensive to get wrong: one calls for out-investing, the other calls for building a company your current company would never approve.

## The question this answers

- Is this entrant a real threat, or a cheaper rival we can out-execute?
- Why do our best customers keep telling us to ignore the thing that later kills us?
- Which of our own funding rules is quietly vetoing the product we will need in five years?
- Do we respond inside the business or stand something up outside it?

## When to run it

Run it when a low-priced entrant is winning accounts you are relieved to lose, when your growth comes from moving upmarket year after year, when a cheap and simple internal idea has died three times for defensible reasons, and once a year as a standing review so results stay comparable.

**Do not run this when** you are the entrant with no market yet — you need a beachhead thesis, not a threat model. Do not run it when the competitor is simply better on the attributes your customers already buy on; that is a sustaining attack, incumbents usually win those, and the answer is to out-invest rather than spin off. And do not run it to justify a project someone already wants funded. "We are disrupting ourselves" is one of the more expensive sentences in corporate strategy.

## The method

**Move 1 — Run the four-part test. All four must hold.**

| Test | Passes if | Fails if |
|---|---|---|
| Underperformance | It is worse on the attribute your mainstream buyers rank first | It is equal or better — that is a sustaining attack |
| Different value | Cheaper, simpler, or more accessible, and it sells to non-consumers or an over-served fringe | It sells the same value to your best accounts at a lower price — that is a price war |
| Asymmetric economics | Its cost structure is profitable at margins that would be a loss for you | It needs your margins to survive — it will run out of money without your help |
| Improvement slope | Its rate of improvement on your primary attribute is steeper than your customers' rate of rising demand | Its slope is flat or slower — it stays in its corner permanently |

Four of four is disruption. Three of four is a watch item with a named trigger and review date. Two or fewer is ordinary competition: respond conventionally and stop using the word, which will otherwise pull the organization toward an expensive spin-off.

The fastest signal, and the one people skip: **check how it feels to lose those deals.** Genuine disruption is experienced at the account level as good news — low-margin, high-support customers walking away. If losing hurts, it is a competitive fight.

**Move 2 — Find the overshoot, because that is what opens the door.**

Disruption lands only when your product has improved faster than customers can absorb. Measure it:

- Share of buyers who select the cheapest tier that clears their requirement. Above 60% is overshoot.
- Adoption of your last three flagship capabilities at twelve months. Below 20% is overshoot.
- Months since a capability feature — not price, not relationship, not integration — decided a competitive deal. Past eighteen, you are selling on something else and have not noticed.

Then locate the market on the buying basis: functionality, then reliability, then convenience, then price. Buyers move to the next criterion once the previous is satisfied by more than one vendor. Entrants come in at exactly the point where the market stops paying for more of what you are best at.

**Move 3 — Audit your own veto machinery.**

The resources are almost never the constraint. Write down the actual numbers:

- What is the minimum gross margin at which a project gets funded here?
- What is the minimum first-year revenue that makes a new line worth an executive's attention?

If your minimum interesting new line is larger than the entrant's entire current market, you cannot pursue that market from inside, and executive enthusiasm will not change it — the resource allocation process will kill the project quietly and for good reasons.

Then test the processes. Take the last three cheap or simple ideas that died internally and reconstruct the exact step that killed each one. Usually it is a market-sizing requirement applied to a market that does not exist yet, or a margin gate applied to a business that will never carry your overhead. That step is your defense mechanism working correctly against your own future.

**Move 4 — Choose the container, then the strategy.**

| Fit | Container |
|---|---|
| Processes fit, values fit | Run it in the core business |
| Processes fit, values do not | Separate P&L with its own margin thresholds and its own approval authority |
| Neither fits | Autonomous unit with its own customers, cost structure, and hiring |

Size the unit so the target market is meaningful to it. A team whose plan needs a few million is thrilled by a market of a few million; a division needing hundreds of millions will strangle the same opportunity with good intentions. Autonomy must include the margin threshold, the comp plan, and the right to sell to customers the core does not want. Anything less is a reporting-line change.

**Move 5 — Plan to learn, not to be right.**

State the two or three numbers the plan depends on and design the cheapest test for each. Treat the first launch as paid market research and budget for two or three wrong market hypotheses. The hard rule: the disruptive product's first market is not a smaller version of your current one. If the launch plan targets your existing customers, you have converted a disruptive product into a sustaining one with worse economics.

## Failure modes

**Disruption as a compliment** — the word attached to any energetic competitor, then used to justify a reorganization. → Run the test and write the score down. Two of four gets a conventional response.

**Waiting until the market is worth entering** — the tell is "we will watch it closely." → By the time it clears your revenue bar, the entrants own the customers at a cost structure you cannot match. Entry timing is set by the improvement slope, not your threshold.

**The captive spin-off** — a separate unit sharing the sales force, the margin targets, and a mandate not to cannibalize. The tell: the new unit inherited the old compensation plan. → Autonomy means separate customers, separate margin gates, separate hiring. Otherwise it is theater with a new logo.

**Asking your best customers** — they will tell you accurately that the cheap product is inadequate, and you will believe them. → They are right about today and useless about the slope. Use mainstream customer input for sustaining decisions only; for disruptive ones, go find the people currently buying nothing.

**Treating it as an engineering problem** — funding a push to make the simple product good enough for the mainstream. → It is a marketing problem. Find the buyers who value the attributes it has right now, in their current, unimpressive form.

**Confusing radical with disruptive** — a large technical leap that serves existing customers better gets treated as a threat. → That is sustaining, and incumbents win those roughly always. Spend the money and win it.

**Cannibalization vetoes** — protecting the cash-generating line. The tell: the phrase "without cannibalizing existing revenue" appears in the strategy document. → If the cannibals are worth blocking, they are already at the door. Decide who eats the business and prefer it to be you.

**Assuming every cheap entrant improves** — a narrative slope with no measurement behind it. → Require an observed rate from release history. Some low-end products never get better, and reacting costs real money.

## Outputs

- A four-part scorecard per credible entrant, with a conventional-vs-disruptive verdict
- Overshoot evidence: tier mix, feature adoption at twelve months, months since capability decided a deal
- Your real margin floor and minimum-interesting-revenue thresholds, in writing
- A post-mortem on three internally killed ideas naming the step that killed each
- A container recommendation with autonomy terms: margin authority, customer scope, hiring, comp
- A discovery plan listing assumptions to test and the budget reserved for wrong hypotheses

## Lineage

Clayton Christensen's *The Innovator's Dilemma* argued from long industry studies that well-managed incumbents fail precisely because they follow good management practice: listen to the best customers, invest where margins are highest, pursue markets large enough to matter. His contribution was showing the failure is rational at every individual decision. This articulation departs from his by making the sustaining-versus-disruptive distinction a hard four-part gate with a stated verdict, because in practice the word is applied to any aggressive competitor and the misdiagnosis costs more than the threat would have. I also put the internal veto audit ahead of the market analysis, since your literal margin floor decides what you are capable of responding to at all.

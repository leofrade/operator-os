# Presentation Craft

> Most bad decks are one artifact trying to be two: a document nobody reads and slides nobody can follow. Pick one before you open the software.

## The question this answers

- Is this read without me in the room, or presented with me in it?
- What is the one thing that survives if they forget everything else?
- Why does a deck full of correct information fail to produce a decision?
- How does a board deck differ from an investor update, or a conference talk?

## When to run it

Run this before building any deck that carries a decision — a board meeting, a fundraise, a pricing change, a strategy review — or any talk the audience chose to attend. Run it on an existing deck when a reviewer calls it "dense" or "hard to follow," which usually means the argument is buried, not that the design is poor.

**Do not run this when** the real gap is the argument. If the strategy is undecided, the numbers unreconciled, or the ask unknown, presentation work only makes a bad case more persuasive — worse than making it clearly. Decide first, present second. Skip it for routine status too: those are documents, and dressing them as presentations wastes an hour of everyone's time.

## The method

### Move 1 — Choose the form before the content

Three forms, three constructions. Choosing wrong is the costliest mistake here, because everything downstream inherits it.

| Form | Read or presented | Density | Test of success |
|---|---|---|---|
| Document | Read, no presenter | Full prose, complete argument | A stranger reaches your conclusion unaided |
| Slides | Presented, presenter carries meaning | One idea per slide, minimal text | Useless without you — that is the point |
| Hybrid deck | Sent ahead, then discussed | Assertion headline plus one exhibit per page | Survives forwarding, still supports discussion |

The hybrid is what most business decks need and almost none are built as. Its rule: each page headline states a claim in a full sentence, and the body is the single exhibit supporting it. Readers get the argument from headlines alone; presenters use the exhibits.

**Exit criterion:** the form is named in writing, and an appendix exists to absorb what it cannot carry.

### Move 2 — Fix the single claim

Write one sentence, under twenty words, stating what you want the audience to believe or do. If you cannot, you are not ready to build. Then state the ask concretely: approve, fund, decide, staff, unblock. A presentation with no ask is an update, and updates belong in email.

**Exit criterion:** claim and ask each fit on one line, and every page either supports the claim or moves to the appendix.

### Move 3 — Structure to the audience's job

Two structures. Using the wrong one is why competent decks stall.

**Answer-first, for decision audiences.** Minto's pyramid: lead with the conclusion, then three supporting arguments, then evidence under each. Boards and investors ration attention and will interrupt; give them the answer while they are still listening. Each level summarizes the one below, and arguments at the same level stay mutually exclusive.

**Tension-first, for persuasion audiences.** Duarte's structure: establish what is, contrast what could be, repeat the gap until it is uncomfortable, then resolve. Use it when the audience does not yet agree there is a problem — conference talks, all-hands, culture change, category creation. Wrong for a board that knows the problem and wants the recommendation.

The tell: someone asks "so what are you recommending?" in the first three minutes. That is an answer-first audience receiving a tension-first build.

### Move 4 — One idea per slide, enforced by the headline

Write every headline as a full-sentence assertion, not a topic label. "Q3 Pipeline" is a filing category. "Pipeline covers 2.1x of the number, so we are short one enterprise deal" is an argument.

A slide whose headline is a complete claim can only hold evidence for that claim. Two ideas force two slides.

```
Slide test — fail any, split or cut
1. Headline is a full sentence making one claim.
2. Everything on the page supports that claim.
3. Read aloud, the headline alone is intelligible.
4. Headlines in sequence read as a coherent argument.
5. Nothing here duplicates what you will say out loud.
```

Item 4 is the strongest check available: extract the headlines into a list. If it does not read as the argument, the deck does not have one.

### Move 5 — Make data comparable and human

A number with no comparison carries no meaning. Every figure gets a prior period, a target, a benchmark, or a unit the audience already feels.

State the takeaway in the headline and let the chart prove it, never the reverse. One chart per page, one message per chart, annotated directly rather than left to inference. A table over about nine cells belongs in the appendix.

### Move 6 — Rehearse the questions, not the script

Rehearse aloud, standing, at full speed, twice minimum. Cut to eighty percent of the slot; discussion is the part that matters and it always overruns.

Then the rehearsal that matters: list the ten hardest questions, write each answer, build the appendix slide behind it. A deep appendix is what lets the main line stay short. Rehearse transitions specifically — presenters know their slides and improvise the joins, which is where arguments come apart.

### Move 7 — Build the named variants to their own rules

**Board deck.** Sent 72 hours ahead; the meeting is discussion, not narration. Structure: decisions requested, metrics against plan with variances explained, risks with owners and dates, then appendix. Nothing in the room should be new — a board learning bad news live discounts everything else you say for a year. Pre-brief the two directors most likely to object.

**Investor update.** Monthly, written, five minutes to read, same section order every time so change is visible at a glance: metrics, what worked, what did not, asks, runway. Lead with the number that moved most, including when it moved down — credibility compounds because bad months are reported in the same voice as good ones.

## Failure modes

**The slideument** — one artifact built to be both read and presented, failing at both. The tell is 75 words on a projected slide. → Build document and slides separately, or build the hybrid deliberately.

**Topic headlines** — every headline is a noun phrase, so the deck files information without arguing anything. The tell is extracted headlines that read as a table of contents. → Rewrite each as a full-sentence claim. This single edit fixes more decks than any design change.

**Narrative build for a decision audience** — you construct toward a reveal and get interrupted at minute three. The tell is being asked for the recommendation before you reach it. → Answer-first. Save the buildup for audiences who chose to attend.

**No appendix** — everything that might be asked is crammed into the main line, so it is unfollowable and you still get caught out. The tell is a 40-slide deck for a 20-minute slot. → Cut to the argument; index the rest as appendix.

**Data without comparison** — figures are presented bare and the audience cannot tell whether they are good. The tell is "compared to what?" from the back of the room. → Every number gets a prior, target, or benchmark, stated in the headline.

**Rehearsing the script** — the presenter memorizes narration and is destabilized by the first interruption. The tell is visible irritation at questions. → Rehearse answers and transitions instead.

**Design before argument** — hours go into typography while the argument is unresolved, because polish is easier than thinking. The tell is a beautiful deck that changes nobody's mind. → Headlines and sequence first; visual work only once the headline list reads as an argument.

**Surprise in the room** — a board or investor meets a material problem live. The tell is a director learning something a phone call could have covered. → Pre-brief. Meetings confirm; they do not reveal.

## Outputs

- A named form — document, slides, or hybrid — with its appendix strategy.
- The single claim in under twenty words, and the explicit ask.
- A headline list, in order, that reads as a coherent argument alone.
- The deck itself, one idea per page, every figure carrying a comparison.
- An indexed appendix mapped to the ten hardest anticipated questions.
- For a board deck: the decisions-requested page and the pre-brief list.
- For an investor update: a fixed-section template reusable monthly.

## Lineage

Nancy Duarte established the case for treating a presentation as a story with structural tension, and the discipline of separating slides from documents. Barbara Minto's *Pyramid Principle* supplies the answer-first construction executive audiences require. Garr Reynolds contributed the restraint argument: subtraction, not addition, is what makes a slide legible.

This articulation departs from all three in one respect and adds one. It rejects the implication that narrative structure is universally superior: for decision audiences, tension-building taxes the listener, so the first structural choice is which audience you have. And it treats the appendix as a first-class instrument rather than an afterthought — the ability to go arbitrarily deep on demand is what earns permission to keep the main argument short.

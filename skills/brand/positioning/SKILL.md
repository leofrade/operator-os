# Positioning

> Positioning is not a paragraph you approve. It is the frame a buyer uses to decide what you should be compared to — and you either choose that frame deliberately or inherit a bad one.

## The question this answers

- Why do prospects who obviously need us keep comparing us to a tool we are not?
- Which market category do we claim, and what do we give up by claiming it?
- Who should we refuse to sell to, even though they would pay?
- We rewrote the site and the deck and nothing moved. Which layer is broken?

## When to run it

Run this when the same objection repeats across unrelated deals — usually "how is this different from X" where X is not someone you consider a competitor. Run it when every rep has invented a personal pitch, before entering a new segment, after the product drifted past the category it was born in, or when a funded entrant redefined the category around you.

**Do not run this when** you have fewer than roughly ten closed customers and no pattern in who succeeds; without that evidence you write a plausible statement and defend it for a year. Run [`discovery/customer-interviews`](../../discovery/customer-interviews/SKILL.md) first. Skip it too when the constraint is pipeline volume — repositioning does not fill an empty calendar, and it is where founders hide from outbound.

## The method

Sequence is the method. Most positioning fails because the team picks a category first — usually the one they can already spell — then reverse-engineers the rest to fit. Each move feeds the next.

### Move 1 — Establish the reference set

Ask what your best customers would have done if you had not existed — in their words, from recorded calls, not a matrix built internally. The list is rarely the funded startups you track. It is a spreadsheet, an internal build, an agency, an analyst working by hand, or absorbing the cost. Rank by frequency of mention, not by how threatening it feels in a board meeting.

**Exit criterion:** the top three were each named out loud by a customer, and at least one is not software. Three venture-backed peers means you asked your own team, not the market.

### Move 2 — Isolate what only you have

List every capability, asset, or structural fact the Move 1 alternatives do not have. Not "better than" — do not have. Include non-product assets: proprietary data, a regulatory approval, an integration nobody else was granted.

Then filter twice. Cut anything you cannot prove in a demo, a document, or a reference call — an unprovable claim is an opinion, and opinions do not position. Then cut anything that only matters after signature; support and reliability drive renewal, not consideration.

**Exit criterion:** three to six attributes survive, each with a named proof artifact.

### Move 3 — Convert attributes into value themes

An attribute is not value. Push each one until it lands on something the buyer already budgets for.

| Attribute | What it makes possible | Value theme |
|---|---|---|
| Only direct regulatory feed | Status known before submission | Cycle time drops from weeks to days |
| Pre-mapped to dominant ERPs | No integration project | Live in a week without borrowing engineers |

Cluster into one to three themes. Three is the ceiling; teams carrying five carry none. Substitution test: if a theme could sit verbatim on a competitor's homepage, it is the price of entry, not a theme. Delete it.

**Exit criterion:** each theme is backed by two attributes and one quantified outcome.

### Move 4 — Name who cares disproportionately

Do not segment by who buys. Segment by who gets an outsized result, then find the observable characteristics that predict it — operating model, regulatory exposure, existing stack, transaction volume. Firmographics matter only as proxies for intensity of pain.

The test: could a stranger use your definition to build a list of fifty qualified accounts without asking you a question? "Mid-market operations leaders" fails. "Multi-site distributors moving hazardous freight across more than two states" passes.

Then pay the cost. Narrowing means declining revenue that would have arrived anyway; the discomfort is the signal you narrowed enough.

### Move 5 — Choose the frame

Ries and Trout's argument is that mental real estate is finite, ranked, and mostly already owned. Buyers hold a short ordered list per category, the first entrant usually keeps the top slot for years, and you do not overwrite an occupied position with a better argument — you find an empty slot or build a new list. Choose only now: the frame follows from Moves 1 through 4.

| Frame | Use when | Real cost | Failure signature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Head-to-head | You beat the leader on criteria buyers already rank | You compete on their scorecard | Win the demo, lose to their checklist |
| Subsegment leadership | A slice has needs the leader structurally will not serve | You cap near-term TAM on purpose | The slice was a feature request |
| A new frame | Existing categories mislead buyers about what you are | You fund buyer education alone | No search volume, no budget line |

Take the third only when you can name the budget line the buyer will use before the category exists, and fund eighteen months of education. Most teams that "create a category" are dodging a comparison they expect to lose. If you do, say what you are not before what you are.

### Move 6 — Test whether the statement is load-bearing

Thirty days after rollout, score it.

```
1. A rep says it unprompted in the first five minutes of discovery.
2. It caused at least one deliberate disqualification this quarter.
3. It changed a roadmap, pricing, or hiring decision within 90 days.
4. A customer restated it accurately to a peer without your materials.
5. It names something you intend to lose to.

5/5  load-bearing — leave it alone for four quarters
3-4  partially adopted — fix rollout and enablement, not the statement
0-2  slogan — return to Move 1; it was written to be approved, not used
```

Item 5 is the one teams resist. Positioning that never loses a deal on purpose is a description written to keep everyone comfortable.

## Failure modes

**Inherited category** — you sit in the category a founder typed into a homepage before the first customer, so it explains the product's origin rather than the buyer's problem. → Rerun Moves 1 and 5 from customer language.

**Aspirational category** — you claim the large adjacent category where you would rank eighth. The tell is losing on a feature checklist you never intended to compete on, repeatedly, at the same stage. → Drop to subsegment leadership. First on a short list beats eighth on a long one.

**Competitor-list narcissism** — the alternatives are three venture-backed peers while real buyers chose between you and a spreadsheet. The tell is messaging that answers questions no buyer asked. → Restrict Move 1 to phrases you can quote from a recording.

**Value-theme laundering** — attributes get renamed into abstractions like "efficiency" and "peace of mind," which pass review because nobody can object. The tell is themes interchangeable with any vendor's. → Apply the substitution test; require a quantified outcome per theme.

**Positioning without distribution change** — the statement ships, the site updates, nothing else moves. The tell is an identical calendar and first-call script a month later. → Not done until the target list, the opening script, and one qualification criterion have changed.

**Line-extension drift** — a new product ships under the established name to borrow trust cheaply and the position blurs. The tell is a sales team needing two minutes to say what the company does. → New position, new name.

**Repositioning treadmill** — the story is rewritten every two quarters, so nothing accumulates. The tell is a third narrative in a year with flat win rates. → Change tactics freely; hold the frame four quarters unless the alternative set moved.

## Outputs

- A ranked competitive-alternatives list in customer language, with quotes and frequency counts.
- The unique-attribute list, each with its proof artifact.
- One to three value themes, each mapped to attributes and a quantified outcome.
- A segment definition specific enough to generate a fifty-account target list.
- The chosen frame, with the rejected options and why.
- A one-page statement plus the load-bearing scorecard scheduled 30 days out.
- A disqualification rule sales can use without escalation.

## Lineage

Al Ries and Jack Trout established that positioning happens in the prospect's head rather than in the product, that attention is rationed, and that an occupied position is cheaper to defend than to take. April Dunford turned that into an executable sequence running from competitive alternatives through unique attributes, value, best-fit segment, and market category. Ries and Trout explain why the frame decides the outcome; Dunford explains how to derive it without guessing.

This articulation departs from both in two ways. It treats category creation as an expensive last resort rather than a peer option. And it adds the load-bearing test: a positioning statement is worthless until it changes what a rep says, who gets disqualified, and what the roadmap funds — the only real difference between positioning and a slogan that survived a review meeting.

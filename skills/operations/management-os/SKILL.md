# Management OS

> Your output is not what you produced this week. It is what your team produced, plus what they will produce because of something you did.

## The question this answers

- I worked sixty hours and my team shipped less than last month. Why?
- How much autonomy do I give this person on this specific task?
- Which meetings survive, and what replaces the ones I delete?
- This report is underperforming. Motivation or training — and how would I know?

## When to run it

Run it when you have crossed from doing to managing and the old habits still
produce your results: calendar full, team waiting, quality dependent on your own
hours. Also when a team passes five people, when a first-time manager takes over,
or when decisions visibly queue behind one person.

**Do not run this when** the real problem is strategy. A team executing hard on
the wrong thing needs a different objective, not better delegation. Skip it too
for teams of two or three founders, where the working management system is a
shared document and a daily conversation.

## The method

### 1. Reprice your hours by leverage

Every managerial hour has a multiplier: how many people's output it changes, and
for how long. Sort your last two weeks into three buckets.

| Leverage | Example | Rough multiple |
|---|---|---|
| High | Teaching a repeatable skill to four people; writing the standard everyone follows; unblocking a stalled decision | 10x, durable |
| Medium | A well-run 1:1; reviewing a plan before work starts, not after | 2–3x |
| Negative | Redoing a report's work; attending as an observer; being the router every message passes through | below 1x, and it trains dependence |

Threshold: below a third of your hours in the high bucket, you do not have a
time-management problem. You have a role problem.

### 2. Set the rope per task, not per person

The wrong question is whether you are hands-on or hands-off. The right one is how
mature this person is on this task — and the same person is a beginner on one
thing and an expert on another in the same week.

| Maturity on the task | Your mode | What you actually do |
|---|---|---|
| New to it | Directive | Structure the work, define done, check at short intervals |
| Doing it, still learning | Coaching | Ask for their recommendation first, then react; review outputs |
| Proven on it | Delegating | Agree the result and the deadline; the method is theirs |

The expensive error is delegating to a beginner in the name of respect; the
customer pays the tuition. The equally expensive error is staying directive with
someone proven, which reads as distrust and drives them out.

### 3. Refuse the next move

When someone brings you a problem and you say "leave it with me," the next move
has moved from their desk to yours. Do that fifteen times and you have a queue,
with fifteen people idle while you work through it.

The rule: no problem leaves the room without the next move belonging to a named
person, with a date. Push everyone up this ladder.

```
LEVEL OF INITIATIVE — floor is 3
1  Wait to be told                         unacceptable
2  Ask what to do                          unacceptable
3  Recommend a course of action, then act on approval   minimum
4  Act, then report immediately            target
5  Act, report in the normal rhythm        for proven work
```

Test that it worked: count the decisions that waited on you each week. If the
number is not falling, you are still accepting the next move.

### 4. Keep four meetings and delete the rest

- **1:1, weekly or fortnightly.** Their agenda, your additions. Mutual
  education: they teach you what is happening below the surface, you transfer
  judgment. Working signal — you stop being first to hear about problems, and
  problems arrive with a proposal attached.
- **Team meeting, weekly.** Coordination and decisions needing more than two
  people. Not status.
- **Operating review, monthly.** Results vs. targets, deviations, corrective
  plans with owners.
- **Goal review, quarterly.** See [`operations/okr-system`](../../operations/okr-system/SKILL.md).

Everything else faces one test: is there a decision to make? If not, it is a
document. Status meetings become a written update read beforehand, or a
dashboard nobody has to attend.

### 5. Diagnose performance with two variables and nothing else

There are only two levers on an individual's output: can they do it, and do they
want to do it.

- Cannot do it → training. Your job, not the HR department's, and the
  highest-leverage hour available to you.
- Can but will not → motivation: usually a broken link between their work and
  something they care about, or a manager they have stopped trusting.

If you cannot say which after a conversation, you have not had the conversation.
A performance improvement plan without the diagnosis is administrative theater.

### 6. Protect the important from the urgent, structurally

Urgent work is loud and makes you feel needed. Important work is silent and
moves the business. Left alone, the calendar fills with the first kind. Fix it
structurally: block the first two hours of the day for the highest-impact task,
batch email into fixed windows, and treat "as soon as possible" as a phrase that
requires a real reason — manufactured urgency propagates downward and costs the
whole team its focus.

Refuse work honestly. Before accepting anything: am I the right person, is this
the right time, do I have enough information? Any no means redirect, not accept
and delay.

## Failure modes

**The bottleneck manager** — everything routes through you because you hold the
context, and you rather enjoy it. Tell: your team's output tracks your
availability; a week of vacation shows up in the numbers. → Raise the initiative
floor to 3, publish the decisions you no longer make, and accept that 70% right
from the team beats 95% right from you two weeks later.

**Busy as a substitute for effective** — full calendar, strong sense of
productivity, flat results. Tell: you cannot name one thing you did last month
that raised the team's permanent capability. → Run the leverage audit. Delete or
delegate every recurring meeting where you are not deciding.

**Delegating to a beginner** — you hand full autonomy to someone new because you
dislike feeling like a micromanager. Tell: the work comes back late and wrong,
and you blame the hire. → Match mode to task maturity. Directive early is
generosity; it is how someone reaches autonomy faster.

**The 1:1 that is a status report** — thirty minutes of ticket-by-ticket
narration you could have read. Tell: you talk more than they do, and nothing
surprising has surfaced in two months. → Their agenda first, opening with what is
not working and what they need from you. Write down commitments; review next time.

**Culture by workaholism** — the people who stay latest get praised, so everyone
stays late. Tell: heroics are celebrated in all-hands and the process failure
behind them never gets fixed. → Praise the removal of the need for heroics. Long
hours are a symptom to diagnose, not a virtue to reward.

**Hiring ahead of the pain** — you hire to fix a problem you have not yet fully
felt. Tell: new people arrive without a clear first-quarter outcome and spend six
weeks looking for work. → Hire when the pain is unmistakable and the role can be
stated as an outcome. Every head adds coordination cost that something must pay
for.

**Standards that live only in heads** — the same task done three ways by three
people. Tell: onboarding takes months and quality depends on who picked up the
work. → Write the standard once, train to it, then improve it.

## Outputs

- A leverage audit of the last two weeks: hours by bucket, negative-leverage
  items marked for deletion or delegation
- A delegation map: each report, their current work streams, and the mode
  (directive / coaching / delegating) for each
- A meeting inventory: what survives, what becomes a document, what is deleted,
  and the decision each survivor exists to make
- A weekly count of decisions that waited on the manager
- Per underperformer, a one-line diagnosis naming training or motivation, and
  the next intervention

## Lineage

The core proposition — that a manager's output is the output of the organization
under their supervision or influence, and that leverage is the unit of
managerial productivity — is Andy Grove's, from *High Output Management*, along
with task-relevant maturity and the taxonomy of process meetings. The delegation
mechanic comes from William Oncken Jr. and Donald Wass, whose *Harvard Business
Review* essay framed the next move as a monkey jumping from one back to another.
The bias against meetings, premature hiring, and long hours as a proxy for
commitment comes from Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson in *Rework*. Where this articulation departs: Grove
wrote for a manager inside a large, stable organization with a working process
layer beneath them. Most operators have no such layer, so I have made the
diagnostic sequence — leverage audit, delegation mode, initiative floor —
mandatory and ordered, and dropped the parts of the tradition that assume
institutional scaffolding you do not have yet.
